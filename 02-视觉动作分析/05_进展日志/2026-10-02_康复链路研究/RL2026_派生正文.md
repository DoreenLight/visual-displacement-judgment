Unlike con- ventional adaptive assist-as-needed controllers based on Iterative Learning Control (ILC-AAN), the proposed RL-AAN controller autonomously adjusts the trade-off between movement errors and robot assistance in response to the user’s recent performance, in real-time, while relying on a small set of high-level tunable parameters that do not require subject-specific manual adjustments. The RL-AAN controller was implemented on a cable-driven, end-effector type rehabilitation robot and validated against a conventional ILC-AAN controller through perturbation- based reaching tasks involving a group of healthy indi- viduals. Compared to ILC-AAN, the RL-AAN controller significantly reduced the amount of robot assistance required during training, promoting user active participa- tion and task performance. Following training with the RL-AAN controller, retention tests showed more accurate arm-reaching trajectories compared to ILC-AAN training, highlighting the potential of RL-AAN for future use in exercise-based rehabilitation. Overall, this work contributes to ongoing research into developing control strategies that enable personalization in physical human-robot interaction (pHRI) and robot-assisted rehabilitation. I NTRODUCTION R EHABILITATION of motor function is a fundamental component of the recovery process for individuals with movement impairments resulting from central or peripheral nervous system injuries (e.g., stroke, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury), neurodegenerative diseases, or muscu- loskeletal injuries [1]. Intense, repetitive, and task-oriented exercises can help individuals with brain injury regain lost motor functions through the process of motor (re)learning [2], [3]. However, traditional rehabilitative interventions are labor- intensive and physically demanding for therapists and face constraints due to limited availability and the high cost of trained personnel, which restrict the frequency and intensity of exercise sessions. Robot-assisted rehabilitation aims to retrain movement planning, control, and execution through exercises mediated by a robotic trainer that delivers controlled assistive or resistive forces, often in conjunction with real-time multisensory feedback, and records quantitative data to inform targeted therapy. This technology has the potential to reduce the physical burden on therapists, optimize human resource allocation, and standardize rehabilitation programs [4], while also empowering patients to engage in self-administered, home-based exercises [5]. Because active e ort is a critical enabler of motor learning [6], it has been postulated that a robotic trainer should only provide the minimum level of assistance required for the patient to accomplish a desired motor task. Thus, an ideal device should be transparent (i.e., exert no force on the user) when the user performs well, and intervene only when the user encounters di culty completing the task, in order to correct excessive movement errors. Control strategies that align with this principle are referred to as “assist-as-needed” (AAN) [7], “patient-in-charge” [8], or “patient-cooperative” [9] con- trollers. Pioneering AAN strategies relied on conventional impedance controllers (IC) with fixed sti ness and damping parameters, implementing a static relationship between move- ment errors and robot assistance [9], [10]. Such controllers require extensive manual tuning as the robot’s compliance must be adjusted to the patient’s varying motor skills over the therapy program [11]. Starting from a naive (untrained) state, an ILC-AAN controller updates the impedance parameter at each movement repetition by scaling the previous impedance parameter by a constant forgetting factor and adding a given function of the kinematic error measured at the previous repetition, multiplied by a constant gain. Other relevant examples of adaptive AAN controllers include: the error-based controller with an AAN force decay term introduced in [14], which modulates robot assistance by learning a model of the patient’s impairment across the robot’s workspace using radial basis functions; the joint-space controller developed in [15], which adjusts IC sti ness based on human net joint torques estimated from on-board load cells via a linear adaptation map; and the optimal controller described in [16], where IC sti ness is set inversely pro- portional to the estimated human joint sti ness, to minimize the weighted sum of kinematic errors and robot’s e ort. While all these approaches can gradually modulate the robot’s assistance as the user’s performances change, they share the following limitations: (i) they assume that the human motor system will always respond to robot-applied forces in the same stereotypical fashion, regardless of the therapy dosage already delivered–that is, the cumulative duration, intensity, and repetition frequency of prior training. This assumption fails to capture the non-linear, session-to-session learning dynamics observed during multi-session exercise, particularly in individuals with sensory or motor impairments; (ii) they are blind to previous movement executions, except for the most recent one; (iii) they operate with a fixed learning update rate, which must be manually adjusted to match the user’s learning pace at di erent stages of the rehabilitation program. To address the limitations of conventional adaptive AAN controllers, more recent studies have aimed to modulate the level of assistance provided by a robotic trainer on-the-fly , using more advanced learning-based control architectures. Sharifi et al. developed a time-varying impedance controller with iterative learning-based adaptation laws to adjust the robot impedance based on human behavior [17]. This method seeks to optimize the balance between movement accuracy and stability, facilitating safe physical human-robot interaction without requiring force / torque measurements. However, it does not directly encourage user participation. Obayashi et al. proposed a task-oriented AAN controller to help users learn a dart-throwing task [18]. The AAN controller adopts a policy- gradient reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm to adjust the IC sti ness based on the most recent throw score. However, this method does not modulate the relative importance of performance versus assistance as training progresses; thus both naive and experienced individuals are challenged using the same predefined control objective. Pareek et al. [19] proposed an RL–based AAN controller for handwriting reha- bilitation, which was evaluated in a convenience sample of healthy individuals. This controller is trained o ine to derive a subject-independent policy that is not updated at runtime. As a result, the policy cannot autonomously adapt to inter-individual variability or session-to-session performance changes without retraining or manual parameter adjustment. Our group introduced the first stride-dependent [20], [21] and phase-dependent [22] RL-AAN controllers for robot assisted gait training, which autonomously reshape the IC of an ankle exoskeleton in real-time based on the wearer’s training performance. Subsequently, other researchers have proposed related RL-based strategies for lower-extremity exoskeletons [23], [24], [25]. Building upon the RL-AAN controller presented in [20] and [21], in this paper we leverage the vast expressive capa- bility of actor-critic RL architectures to model the complex, subject-specific co-adaptation processes originating from the human-robot coupled system during repeated robot-assisted arm-reaching exercises. Although RL has been applied to functional electrical stimulation (FES) controllers for exercise- based rehabilitation and, more recently, to a variety of upper- and lower-limb assistive and rehabilitation robots—including powered orthoses, prostheses, and end-e ector-based devices [26]—work on RL-based AAN control remains limited. To the best of our knowledge, this paper introduces the first on- line RL-based AAN control strategy for rehabilitation robots explicitly designed to promote both movement accuracy and user active e ort during upper-limb reaching exercises. From an algorithmic standpoint, the new RL-AAN controller extends previous research by explicitly accounting for the user active e ort in the actor’s cost function, by modulating the bal- ance between movement errors and robot assistance directly, through the actor’s control objective, and by autonomously varying the task complexity in response to the subject’s performance over recent movement repetitions following a performance-based curriculum learning scheme [27], [28], [29]. The resulting RL-AAN controller relies on a minimal set of subject-independent parameters, allowing for intuitive tuning and fast convergence. To demonstrate feasibility, we compared the e ectiveness of the new RL-AAN controller to a conventional ILC-AAN controller, in terms of human motor adaptation during and immediately following sessions of robot-assisted arm-reaching tasks, which were administered using a custom-engineered cable-driven parallel robot (CDPR). The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section II intro- duces the experimental setup, including the CDPR setup and the implementation of robot-assisted arm-reaching exercises; Section III describes the proposed RL-AAN controller and the conventional ILC-AAN controller (used for comparison) which modulate the CDPR’s impedance during the arm- reaching exercises; Section IV deals with the experimental protocol used for validation with healthy individuals, and the statistical analysis. Results are presented in Section V and discussed in Section VI. Finally, conclusions and future work are presented in Section VII. II. ILC-AAN Controller ILC is often utilized in robotic applications involving repetitive tasks. ILC aims to improve task execution by grad- ually adjusting the control action after each task repetition, leveraging feedback from previous repetitions [12]. In the present implementation, a P-type ILC-AAN controller [7], [37] adjusts the impedance parameter g to be applied during the next movement repetition (hereafter referred to as “cycle”) according to g k + 1 = ILC g k + f ( d k ) , (4a) f ( d k ) = K d k b , (4b) where k is the index of the most recently completed cycle, f ( d ) is a linear function of the adjusted movement error d , with gain K > 0 and intercept b , and ILC ∈ (0 , 1) is the forgetting factor. Appropriate tuning of ILC , K , and b is critical to ensure adaptability to user performance and promote active participation. The forgetting term ILC must be adjusted to prevent the user from over-relying on the CDPR assistance, thereby reducing their own e ort [38]. Indeed, ILC determines the rate at which the learning algorithm reduces g when the movement error is small, steering the CDPR towards a weaker assistive force field (2), with the e ect of challenging the user. This fading assistance has positive e ects in terms of motor learning [39]. If K is not properly tuned in relation to ILC , the controller may not be able to compensate for the e ect of the forgetting factor. This could decrease g even when the error is substantial, or, conversely, saturate g even at small errors, e ectively leading to a static IC. First, each participant underwent a 60-cycle familiarization session (FAM), consisting of 30 arm-reaching repetitions without F pert , and 30 repetitions with F pert , spanning all six directions, in random order. This session was carried out with null F AAN to let the participant familiarize themselves with the task and the type of perturbation generated by the CDPR. Next, participants completed a baseline session (BSLN) consisting of 60 arm-reaching cycles (10 along each direction, in random sequence) performed with null F AAN and F pert , to evaluate their unperturbed / unassisted task performance. Subsequently, participants underwent a 60-cycle pre-training (PRE) session, during which they were exposed to F pert without receiving external assistance. The purpose of this session was to evaluate how each participant responded to unexpected disturbances. Data collected from this session was also compared to post- training data to evaluate any training e ects. Additionally, for the RL-AAN controller, the PRE session was used to pre-train the critic NN, starting from a naive state and setting g 0 in place of the actor output. This approach improves data e - ciency by allowing the critic NN to observe the environment under a transparent policy [19]. Afterwards, participants took part in 4 training sessions (T-1 to T-4), each consisting of 180 cycles (i.e., 30 cycles along each direction, in random order) with both F pert and F AAN active. The RL-AAN and ILC-AAN controllers adapted cycle-by-cycle throughout the entire training period (T-1 to T-4) without being reset. Five- minute breaks were included in-between PRE and T-1 to T-4 sessions to minimize physical and mental fatigue. To assess the development of internal feed-forward models in the study participants, 12 catch trials – evenly distributed across the 6 movement directions – were administered during each training session. Specifically, one catch trial was randomly inserted into each 30-cycle block by unexpectedly removing both F AAN and F pert , as described in [47]. These 12 catch trials were conducted in addition to the 180 cycles that comprised each training session. Finally, training e ects were assessed during three post-training sessions (PT1 to PT3), each including 60 cycles with F pert but no assistance. VI. D ISCUSSION In recent years, there has been increasing attention on rehabilitation technologies that enable the individualization of therapy procedures [50]. Traditionally, generalized con- trol approaches have been deployed with limited success [38]; however, acknowledging the variability in individuals’ behavioral profiles has unveiled new possibilities for robot- assisted treatment [51], [52], [53]. Robotic technologies are well-positioned to continuously evaluate an individual’s motor performance and adjust exercise challenge levels accordingly, thereby promoting sustained engagement [54]. Integrating per- sonalization in robot-assisted exercise training may facilitate the development of internal models, enhancing the e ective- ness of rehabilitation therapies [55], [56]. This study introduced a new RL-based AAN controller for robot-assisted arm-reaching exercises, demonstrating that the new RL-AAN controller outperforms a conventional ILC- based adaptive controller in achieving the primary objective of AAN control: to self-adapt the level of assistance provided by a rehabilitation robot to an individual’s performance, o ering only the minimal assistance needed for them to complete

<!-- Page 9 -->

540 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON NEURAL SYSTEMS AND REHABILITATION ENGINEERING, VOL. 34, 2026 the motor task, to encourage active participation and promote motor adaptation. The conventional ILC-AAN controller relies on a simplistic law that only considers the most recent movement execution and always responds in the same stan- dardized way to the user’s motor behaviors, irrespective of the amount of training received. Conversely, the proposed RL-AAN controller encourages active participation at three di erent levels. First, unlike ILC-AAN, RL-AAN considers multiple error components in its states. By distinguishing overcompensation (¯ e neg ) from undercompensation (¯ e pos ), it can track user engagement after each movement execution and more e ectively identify and exploit hidden patterns relating user participation to long-term performance. Second, the actor NN continuously seeks an optimal policy minimizing a varying cost that embeds both measures of an individual’s engagement (physical e ort) and estimates of their future motor behaviors (kinematic errors), leveraging the user’s performance history to modulate the relative importance between the two contri- butions via the control objective U c . Finally, the RL model features a performance-based curriculum learning structure [27], [28], [29], which adjusts the level of task di culty by dynamically modifying the level of acceptable error [21], thereby allowing the RL-AAN controller to not only adjust the assistance level but also to scale task di culty based on user performance. This curriculum-learning-based adaptation mechanism promotes sustained engagement and may facilitate motor learning by keeping the task within the user’s zone of proximal development, i.e., the space between what a learner can do without assistance and what they can do with assistance [57]. Overall, the LMM analysis indicated that the RL-AAN controller induced an increasing trend in user engagement during training, resulting in better retention after training compared to the conventional ILC-AAN controller. Two studies on RL-based controllers for robot-assisted upper-limb rehabilitation incorporated minimization of robotic assistance into their model design [18], [19]. While both studies reported improvements in error reduction, they did not include a quantitative analysis of user engagement during training and retention sessions, nor did they administer catch trials to validate the establishment of internal models. In this context, the findings of the present study provide new insights, supporting our initial hypothesis that the enhanced adaptability of the RL-AAN controller may lead to more e cient learning compared to the conventional ILC-AAN method, as evidenced by improved human motor adaptation dynamics during the exercise and more accurate retention following training. Due to its limited adaptability, the ILC-AAN controller is intrinsically di cult to fine-tune to an individual. The optimal forgetting factor and error gain can vary significantly across subjects, and must be determined by trial-and-error. This is an extremely tedious task, as it requires multiple rounds of testing and validation, with su cient washout periods in- between trials. For this reason, most studies on the topic have adopted one-size-fits-all settings [7], [13]. In contrast, the proposed RL-AAN controller achieves personalization, without requiring individualized tuning, by incorporating the control objective U c and the high-level performance parameter . This approach accounts for user engagement and simpli- fies the model’s tuning process. This architecture provides a leveled structure ensuring that both the actor and critic NNs converge rapidly, significantly enhancing the model’s real- time adaptability to individual behaviors, and eliminating the need for manual tuning. Furthermore, U c and facilitate more intuitive performance monitoring and system adjustments. This simplified tuning process o ers an advantage not only over traditional ILC-based controllers, but also over more recent learning-based models. While RL-based algo- rithms generally require less parameters to be manually tuned compared to traditional ILC-based controllers, they also neces- sitate large pre-training datasets [18], [58], making them less practical for real-world applications. For example, the AR3n controller proposed in [19] requires a considerable amount of pre-training data and a simulated training environment, which adds another layer of complexity. In contrast, the proposed RL-AAN controller takes advantage of a curriculum-learning- based design that does not require any large pre-trained dataset. The critic NN converges during the PRE session, significantly lowering the demand of data and mitigating par- ticipant burden. In summary, through continuous re-balancing of kinematic error and robotic e ort via the actor cost U c , the proposed RL-AAN controller achieves user adaptation beyond what is a orded by traditional ILC laws and o ine RL methods. The present study also highlighted several limitations of the proposed RL-AAN framework. First, the acceptable error thresholds ( low , med , and high ) were manually set to uniform values across all participants, and the network learning rates ( c , a ) were defined from pilot data. This approach intro- duces subjectivity and may not generalize well to users with heterogeneous functional impairments. Developing automated methods to tune these thresholds and adapt learning rates based on individual performance would enhance both objectivity and generalizability. Second, future work should include a sensitivity analysis to examine the relative impact of the RL- AAN parameters in Tab. I in terms of user performance. In terms of experimental design, incorporating a control condition with visual feedback but no physical guidance would be valuable for elucidating how RL- and ILC-based assistance compare to unassisted practice. While this study suggests the e ectiveness of the RL-AAN algorithm in inducing short-term motor adaptations in healthy individuals, additional work is required before this approach can be piloted in individuals post-stroke. Stroke survivors often exhibit hemiparetic weakness, spasticity, abnormal mus- cle synergies, reduced shoulder and elbow range of motion, and sensory deficits —factors that can profoundly influence reaching task performance [59]. Notably, substantial direction- dependent di erences in transverse-plane reaching have been reported in this population. Diagonal movements toward the contralateral-front quadrant and back toward the ipsilateral- back quadrant appear the most sensitive to impairment level [60], likely due to common deficits in elbow-shoulder coor- dination (e.g., flexion synergy). By contrast, findings for anteroposterior and mediolateral reaching tasks have been mixed [61], [62]. Therefore, future work involving stroke survivors should incorporate larger samples and increased repetitions per target to examine whether movement direction moderates performance during and after RL-AAN training.

Furthermore, since rehabilitation gains are largely task-specific [2], further research is warranted to explore the application of the presented RL-AAN strategy to robotic platforms that enable reaching tasks beyond the transverse-plane motions investigated in this paper, as well as those enabling training of the distal upper limb [64]. VII. C ONCLUSION This work introduced the first on-line RL-based assist-as- needed strategy explicitly designed to promote both movement accuracy and user active e ort during upper-limb reach- ing exercises. The proposed RL-AAN controller employs an ADHDP framework to modulate the level of robotic assis- tance based on the user’s recent performance. Unlike other RL-based controllers for upper-limb rehabilitation, the pro- posed controller dynamically adjusts the balance between user performance and e ort in the actor cost, as well as the task di culty, to encourage active participation and promote motor adaptation. The RL-AAN controller was validated against a con- ventional ILC-AAN controller through tests with healthy individuals using a cable-driven rehabilitation robot. During training, the RL-AAN controller outperformed the ILC-AAN controller by eliciting similar levels of movement accuracy on the user while applying substantially lower assistance. Furthermore, study participants demonstrated more accurate retention of the target movement after training with the RL-AAN controller. This work contributes to ongoing research demonstrating the benefits of personalization in pHRI and robot-assisted training, paving the way for future validation studies with brain-injured populations.
