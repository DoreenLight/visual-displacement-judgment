# Test-retest reproducibility of elbow goniometric measurements in a rigid double-blinded protocol: Intervals for distinguishing between measurement error and clinical change

Berry Cleffken, MD,<sup>a</sup> Gerard van Breukelen, PhD,<sup>b</sup> Henk van Mameren, MD, PhD,<sup>c</sup> Peter Brink, MD, PhD,<sup>a</sup> and Steven Olde Damink, MD, MSc, PhD,<sup>a</sup> Maastricht, The Netherlands

Increasingly, goniometry of elbow motion is used for qualification of research results. Expression of reliability is in parameters not suitable for comparison of results. We modified Bland and Altman’s method, resulting in the smallest detectable differences (SDDs). Two raters measured elbow excursions in 42 individuals (144 ratings per test person) with an electronic digital inclinometer in a classical test-retest crossover study design. The SDDs were $O \pm 4 . 2 ^ { \circ }$ for active extension; $O \pm 8 . 2 ^ { \circ }$ for active flexion, both without upper arm fixation; $0 \pm 6 . 3 ^ { \circ }$ foractive extension; $O \pm 5 . 7 ^ { \circ }$ foractive flexion; $O \pm 7 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ for passive flexion with upper arm fixation; $0 \pm \ 1 0 .$ 1<sup>-</sup> for active flexion with upper arm retroflexion; and $0 \pm 8 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ and $O \pm \nobreakspace 1 0 . 8 ^ { \circ }$ for active and passive range of motion. Differences smaller than these SDDs foundin clinicalorresearch settings are attributable to measurement error and do not indicate improvement. (J Shoulder Elbow Surg 2007;16:788-794.)

A major proportion of daily practice of orthopaedic surgeons concerns fracture treatment and mobility problems of joints. Valid evaluation of therapeutic success is paramount. Established measures for treatment outcome are duration of fracture healing, number of delayed unions, pseudarthrosis and infections, pain scores, weight-bearing capacity, and range of motion (ROM) of the treated and adjacent joints. Objective assessment of joint motion is essential for identification of treatment failure and the need for therapeutic intervention.

The gold standard for evaluation of joint motion is measurement of maximum joint excursion by radiography,<sup>6,7,23</sup> which has the disadvantage of being time-consuming and exposes patients to radiation. In clinical practice, the most used noninvasive alternative to x-ray measurement of joint angles is goniometry. Both traditional parallelogram goniometers and sophisticated forms like gravity goniometers are widely used. 14

In research settings, digital gravity goniometry is increasingly applied for expression of success of treatment, and good levels of reproducibility have been claimed.<sup>2,21,22</sup> However, the statistical methods used to express intra- and inter-rater reproducibility in these studies (Pearson’s correlation coefficient, intraclass correlation coefficient, or the coefficient of variation) are merely statistical tools to express correlation but lack clinical applicability. In the present study, we studied the intra- and inter-rater reproducibility of gravity goniometry in elbow measurements, using a rigid double-blinded protocol. Moreover, we introduce a statistical approach that expresses reproducibility in a clinically applicable way, defining reproducibility for digital goniometry in confidence limits. These confidence limits can be used for reference in future research settings. Differences found with digital goniometry smaller than these intervals should be interpreted with caution, as these are most likely the result of measurement error.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Subjects

The sample size calculated for a 90% power of testing and a 1% level of significance with a true correlation of $. 6 0 \ | \alpha = . 0 1 , \beta = . 1 \bar { 0 } |$ ) was a minimum of 30 subjects.<sup>11</sup> In the present study, 42 healthy volunteers were recruited (23 males; mean age, 22.1 years; range, 19-27 years). Par ticipation followed informed consent. Criteria for exclusion were previous or current complaints of the elbow or shoulder symptoms and previous injuries or prior operations on the elbow or shoulder. Left- and right-handed persons were measured on the dominant side. 15

## Protocol

We used the classical test-retest crossover design (Figure 1) that was executed by 2 raters (A and B), who were ex perienced users and meticulously trained with the device and the protocol. Both raters and test subjects were blinded to the results of measurements. Subjects were studied during 4 sessions for each rater, with 2 sessions being executed on the first measurement day and 2 sessions after 2 days at the same hour of the day (Figure 1). During each session, 3 measurements were performed for each joint excursion, resulting in a total of 144 measurements per test subject. Subjects were studied on the same day by both raters (1-hour rest in between). The rater performing the 1st measurements session remained constant for each subject but was equally dis tributed between subjects, after randomization by sealed envelope drawing (rater A was first in 22 subjects and rater B in 20).

## Materials

Joint excursion was measured using the electronic digital inclinometer (EDI) type EDI 320 (Cybex Inc, Ronkonkoma, NY). This device is gravity dependent and indicates motion on a 360<sup>-</sup> scale. The EDI 320 consists of a hand-held unit and a portable display unit with an integral rechargeable power source (Figure 2). The EDI 320 has 3 measurement sequences: continuous mode for continuous registration, single reference mode for recording gross motion, and com pound reference mode that allows determination of separate angles within a greater range of motion (ie, precise joint angle detection). In the present study, all measurements were performed in the single reference mode for joint position measurements.

## Setting and standardization

The rating and data collection sessions were held at a uni versity kinematics science laboratory. A practice session was held to establish a standardized testing procedure. Measurements were performed at right angles to the gravity field at all times. In order to standardize testing procedures, anatomic reference points were marked with a skin marker with the elbow flexed at 90<sup>-</sup>. Reference points were 3 cm dis tal to the olecranon on the dorsal surface of the ulna and 3 cm proximally at the imaginary line in between epicon dyles.<sup>19</sup> Before the measurements of joint excursion were performed, full joint motion was executed 3 times to diminish stretching and learning biases.<sup>3</sup>

## Measurements

Elbow ROM is influenced by movements of the shoulder joint.<sup>18,20,24</sup> Fixation of the upper arm should hypothetically result in diminished shoulder movements and, therefore, im prove reproducibility. To evaluate the need for fixation of the upper arm, 3 methods for testing active maximum flexion were evaluated. The first method (A) consisted of a setting with the upper arm fixed to a fixation block and the test persons in sitting position with the shoulder in a horizontal plane to the fixation block (20 x 22 x 15 cm), resulting in 90<sup>-</sup> forward flexion of the shoulder. The EDI was placed on the dorsal surface of the distal upper arm (Figure 3). The second method (B) was with the test persons standing straight. The upper arm was placed adducted against the body with the shoulder in 0<sup>-</sup> flexion, and with the hand and forearm in midpronation. The elbow was flexed from this position while keeping the upper arm in the same position (Figure 4). An alternate method (C) of measurement (theoretically resulting in larger maximum flexion by stretching the biceps brachii) was performed with the shoulder in retrofexion, without ab duction and rotation.<sup>18,20</sup> Again, subjects were supine and flexion was accomplished with the upper arm held still (Fig ure 5). Measurement of elbow active maximum flexion and passive maximum flexion was performed from a starting position of active maximum extension and vice versa, as this has been shown to result in the greatest joint excursion. Pas sive maximum flexion was only determined by method A because it is impossible to keep the upper arm and shoulder in the same position while applying force to the forearm.

Pearson’s r considers random, but not systematic, error between test scores.<sup>9,10</sup> High reproducibility depends on small random error and absence of systematic error between repeated measurements.<sup>17</sup> The ICC takes both random and systematic errors into account and is, therefore, a better measure than Pearson’s correlation.<sup>9</sup> However, both measures strongly depend not only on the amount of systematic and random error, but also on the heterogeneity of the study population,<sup>8,13</sup> which makes it difficult to compare different reliability studies and use these coefficients for clinical decision making. We, therefore, prefer to use the limits of agreement as proposed by Bland<sup>5</sup> and Altman, which is based on the mean and standard devation (SD) of the difference between two ratings of the same patient and, thereby, has 3 advantages. First, it does not systemat ically depend on the heterogeneity of the study population. Second, it quantifies the amount of measurement error in units of the measurement scale used, for example, in degrees. Third, it is only minimally influenced by the number of raters for determination of inter-rater reliability.

A measure of agreement must indicate the possible size of the difference between 2 raters (or ratings by the same rater) due to systematic as well as random measurement errors. The mean difference between 2 raters, if statistically significant, indicates systematic error in either rater. The SD of the difference between raters indicates the amount of random error, since the variance of this difference is equal to the sum of the measurement error variance of rater A and that of rater B. Bland and Altman proposed to calculate the 95% limits of agreement (mean of the difference 6 1.96\* SD), which indicates the maximum difference between raters that can be expected due to systematic and random measurement error.<sup>1,4,5</sup> This Bland and Altman method can be visualized by plotting the difference between the first and second ratings against the mean of the first and second ratings (Figure 6). Apart from sampling error, the mean of the N individual differences between the 2 raters must be zero, unless there is a systematic difference between both raters. This can be tested with Student’s paired t test. If the mean difference is significant, then the confidence limits of agreement must take this into account, as already done by the Bland and Altman method. However, if these limits are to be applied in clinical practice with other raters than the 2 under study, then the sign of the mean difference $( \mathsf { m } \mathsf { D } > 0 \mathsf { o r } < 0 )$ is not known beforehand.

Taking the mD of our raters as best guess of the mean dif ference that may be expected to occur in clinical practice with other raters of similar experience and training, and tak ing into account that the sign of the mD (>0 or <0) for 2 such other raters is not known beforehand, we obtain the follow ing 95% confidence limits for individual differences between 2 raters:-jmDj - 1.96\*SD to +jmDj + 1.96\*SD.

These corrected confidence limits can also be termed the smallest detectable difference (SDD), as they represent the (95%) threshold for change that can be detected by the EDI 320 beyond systematic and random measurement error.<sup>4,9</sup> These intervals assume that, apart from a possible systematic difference reflected by a mD unequal to zero, the raters are equally reliable. Consequently, the intrarater correlation between 2 measurements should be about the same for both raters. This equality of intrarater correlations and SDs between raters was confirmed by the data (see, for example, the Appendix, where raters A and B have very similar SDs and correlations).

Reproducibility was determined on the basis of (a) the mean of all 3 measurements per patient per session (in the sequel denoted as m3) and (b) the first measurement per patient per session (denoted 1 m). Note that m3 will give higher a higher reproducibility than 1 m due to the Spearman Brown effect, that is well known in psychometric literature. Using each of these 2 bases, the following intra- and inter rater comparisons were made to evaluate reproducibility.

Intrarater. Same day (for each day per rater: mD, SD, SDD and r between both sessions for this rater on this day were computed, followed by taking the average of each sta tistic over days) (Table 1 and Appendix). Different day ses sions (ie, measurement session 1 vs 3, 1 vs 4, 2 vs 3, 2 vs 4), source of disagreement: random errors. Extreme sessions (using only the first and the last measurement session, ie,1 and 4), sources of disagreement: random errors plus time effects that may occur between repeated visits of a patient to the same clinician.

Inter-rater. Same sessions (for each of the 4 sessions per rater, mD, sd, SDD, and r between raters were computed, and each of these statistics was then averaged over the 4 ses sions, ie, session 1 rater A vs 1 rater B, 2 vs 2, 3 vs 3, 4 vs 4), sources of disagreement: random errors plus systematic dif ference between raters. First sessions, sources of disagree ment: as for same sessions, but first sessions may be clinically more realistic (ie, using only: session 1 rater A vs 1 rater B). Extreme sessions (ie, using only: session 1 rater A vs 4 rater B, session 4 rater A vs 1 rater B), sources of disagreement: random errors plus systematic difference between raters plus time effects between repeated visits of a patient.

The data were analyzed using SPSS version 12.0 (Statistics Package for the Social Sciences, SPSS Inc, Chicago, Il). For each outcome, the presence of a systematic mean differ ence between raters, or between sessions within a rater, was tested with the Student t test for paired samples, using a 2- tailed a of 5%. Although the risk of a type I error (ie, a false significance) is increased by multiple testing, we did not choose a smaller a to prevent type II errors (ie, false nonsigni ficance), because a type II error would lead to a SDD that is too small.

Additionally, we tested the mean outcome difference be tween male and female patients with the Student t test for un paired samples, using as outcome the average of the 24 (8 sessions, \* 3 measurements) replications per person.

Note that the presence of such a difference has no impli cation for the calculation of the SDDs, as SDDs are based on intrapatient differences between replications and, thereby, unaffected by the amount of variation between patients. In contrast, measures such as the ICC and Pearson’s correla tion become higher as variation between patients in creases. 8

## DISCUSSION

The main aim of our study was to find clinical application for reproducibility in goniometric readings with an EDI 320 gravity goniometer in a simple protocol. The present data show that good standardization of a goniometric measurement protocol results in highly comparable absolute intra-rater $\mathsf { S D D s }$ for both raters. These are also explained by the fact that we used healthy young individuals to exclude variability, and we minimized learning and stretching bias by a premeasurement practice session. No statistical significant difference in SDDs of active maximum extension was found for methods of measurement with or without fixation of upper arm. However, we did find a difference in active maximum extension of $2 . 7 ^ { \circ }$ between methods, with nonfixation leading to further joint excursion (Table II). This may be explained by placement of the EDI 320 goniometer on the dorsal side with muscle relaxation in method A opposed to method B, where there is no full relaxation of the triceps.

Method B resulted in higher reproducibility than method C for measurements of active maximum flexion $( \mathsf { S D D } 0 \pm 8 . 2 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { v s } 0 \pm 1 0 . 1 ^ { \circ } )$ ). Method B also rendered further active maximum flexion than method C $( 1 3 7 . 2 ^ { \circ } \lor \mathfrak { s } \ 1 3 5 . 5 ^ { \circ } )$ , although clearly not statistically significant. This rejects the theory that elbow flexion with the shoulder in retroflexion would result in greater excursion due to the stretch on the biceps.

Fixation of the upper arm (method A) narrowed confidence limits with $2 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ and 4.4<sup>-</sup> as opposed to methods B and C for active maximum flexion. Our results suggest that elbow joint motion should be measured using fixation of the upper arm. It can be hypothesized that this method would even improve reproducibility more when used in the elderly, but this needs further study. A small difference in reproducibility was found for active maximum flexion versus passive maximum flexion SDDs (confidence limits 0 6 $5 . 7 ^ { \circ } \mathsf { v s } 0 \pm 7 . 4 ^ { \circ } )$ . As expected, maximum joint excur sion was larger for passive maximum flexion (156.5<sup>-</sup> vs 150.7<sup>-</sup>). 12

Calculation of reproducibility using the mean of 3 measurements resulted in narrowing of the SDD with $1 . 1 ^ { \circ } \cdot 4 . 2 ^ { \circ }$ , consequently, also improving reproduc ibility. Using only 1 measurement for clinical practice reduces reproducibility and, with it, the power to detect true alterations in capacity for joint motion in patients, due to the Spearman-Brown effect .<sup>8</sup>

Calculation of the SDD using Bland-Altman’s method, as used in this study, is useful for clinical prac tice with individual patients. In contrast to reproducibility measures like Pearson’s r or ICC, the SDD can be used in clinical practice, as it expresses the threshold for change that can be detected by the EDI 320 beyond measurement error. SDD is, therefore, a clinically applicable statistic that gives the physician a strong tool that can be used to determine if the change in joint excursion of an individual patient is based on random error of the measurement or based on therapeutic success or failure. It can be hypothesized that determination of SDDs in clinical practice will result in broader confidence limits, as ours were the result of strict standardization in young, healthy volunteers without joint complaints.

As expected, we found better intra- than inter-rater reproducibility scores. Not surprisingly, our data show that the ideal evaluation of a change in joint motion is by measurements performed by the same rater at a fixed time using a standardized protocol, perform ing at least 3 measurements. However, in clinical prac tice, revision is not always by the same rater.

As mentioned, goniometry is increasingly used for qualification of therapeutic success in literature. For future research studies with use of digital goniometry, conclusions about improvement, even if the result of statistical testing between two intervention groups, should be made with caution. A statistical difference for alteration in joint motion in degrees smaller than the SDDs presented can technically not be measured with digital goniometry, as it is within the smallest detectable difference for the method of measurement. We advise use of the SDDs in Table I for judgements about clinical change. Differences smaller than these SDDs are probably attributable to measurement error and do not indicate improvement.
