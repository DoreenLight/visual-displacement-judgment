## a b s t r a c t

Study Design: This is a reliability study using the intraclass correlation coefficient. Purpose: The purpose of this study was to determine whether an individual with minimal training could use the iPhone Level application to self-measure the range of motion of the forearm and wrist from a remote location. Methods: Forty healthy participants (80 wrists) were measured twice by two examiners using a universal goniometer and the iPhone Level application. After measurement, each participant received a training session in the self-measurement method. They were then asked to perform remote self-measurements two to three days later and report their findings to the examiners using Skype or FaceTime. Results: SPSS, version 26, was used to run intraclass correlation coefficients using a two-way random analysis at a 95% confidence interval with absolute agreement. Comparisons of single measurements were used to determine reliability. Good inter-rater reliability was found between wrist flexion and extension in all testing conditions. Measurement of active motion in supination, pronation, radial, and ulnar deviation demonstrated moderate reliability compared with the universal goniometer where the measurements were performed by the investigators. Self-measurement of the participant resulted in moderate reliability for supination and poor reliability in pronation, radial, and ulnar deviation. Discussion: Some participants found the procedures technologically and perceptually challenging. Anatomical variances, positional requirements, and substitution patterns complicated the process. Conclusion: The iPhone Level application may be used to perform reliable self-measurements of wrist flexion and extension from a remote location. Further research exploring methods for remote selfmeasurement is indicated.

The measurement of range of motion (ROM) is an important component of examination following wrist injury. Traditionally, the universal goniometer has been the gold standard used by therapists to perform this function. Recently, there has been an increased focus on the use of mobile devices and their ability to reliably measure joint motion. The iPhone Level Application is a free, standard feature, located within the “Measure" application on all iPhones. A study was conducted on normal wrists to explore the inter-rater reliability of the iPhone Level application in the measurement of the forearm and wrist active range of motion compared with the universal goniometer. This study also sought to determine whether an individual with minimal training could perform measurements on themselves from a remote location two to three days following the instruction.

## Methods

Forty healthy participants (80 wrists), 30 females and 10 males, aged 18 to 63 years took part in the study (Table 1). The participants were required to be aged 18 years or older, provide written consent, and have access to an iPhone and an additional electronic device with remote capabilities. Individuals with acute injury to the wrists or forearms, pain in the upper extremity, or an onset of injury between the test and retest were excluded from the study.

In addition, participants were excluded if they possessed previous knowledge or training in goniometry. The measurements were performed by a certified hand therapist with 29 years of experience and an occupational therapy student who had completed training in goniometry. Good reliability was established between the investigators before data collection. Each participant was measured by both examiners using a 6-inch plastic $1 8 0 ^ { \circ }$ goniometer and the iPhone Level application. The participant's own iPhone was used when obtaining measurements. The model of phone was not controlled to explore ecological validity (Table 2). Preconditioning passive and active range of motion exercises were performed before measurement. The motions measured were supination, pronation, wrist flexion, extension, and radial and ulnar deviation. Wrist flexion and extension were performed using the volar and dorsal methods with the elbow at $9 0 ^ { \circ }$ of flexion and the forearm resting on an arm rest of a chair to control rotation of the forearm and ensure consistency during self-measurement (Figs. 1 and 2). Radial and ulnar deviation were performed with the forearm perpendicular to the floor and resting on the wall for the Level application to function properly (Figs. 3 and 4). Pronation and supination were performed in a seated position with the elbow flexed to 90 degrees and the shoulder adducted to the side (Figs. 5 and 6). To keep the procedures consistent, the testing positions were the same for both measuring techniques. Each motion was repeated in between measurements. The results for goniometry were blinded to the examiner and recorded by a separate reader who had also been trained in goniometry. After the assessment, the participants were provided with written handout and training. They then demonstrated the technique to the examiners. A remote session using Skype or Facetime was conducted two to three days after initial assessment and selfmeasurements were obtained.

## Discussion

Recent studies have reported the reliability of various smartphone applications in measuring range of motion. Most of these investigated the ability of a trained examiner to perform the measurements (Keogh et al 2019, Pourahmadi et al 2017).<sup>1,2</sup> Few studies have examined whether individuals unfamiliar with goniometry could achieve similar results. Modest et al (2018)<sup>3</sup> found good reliability in the self-measurement of the wrist flexion, extension, supination, and pronation when the examiner was physically present and simultaneously performing goniometry. This study expands on these findings, and the results indicate that individuals with uninjured wrists can perform reliable selfmeasurement of wrist flexion and extension from a remote location without the physical presence of an examiner. However, unlike previous studies, supination, pronation, and radial and ulnar deviation were not as reliable.

Differences in statistical analysis and parameters selected to determine reliability may contribute to this variance. Limitations to this study include the decreased generalizability of its findings as it was performed on normal wrists. Individuals with injuries would demonstrate more variability secondary to factors such as swelling and pain. Therefore, the results do not apply to injured wrists and forearms. Some participants found the procedure complicated and reported perceptual and technological difficulties when performing the self-measurements.

Anatomical variations and the hand size also affected the placement of the phone in some individuals.

## Conclusion

The ability of a person to self-monitor improvements in range of motion at home might result in increased motivation and compliance to home exercise programs. In addition, with the growing focus on telehealth, further research exploring the reliability of this method on injured individuals as well as identifying other assessments that can be performed remotely is indicated.
