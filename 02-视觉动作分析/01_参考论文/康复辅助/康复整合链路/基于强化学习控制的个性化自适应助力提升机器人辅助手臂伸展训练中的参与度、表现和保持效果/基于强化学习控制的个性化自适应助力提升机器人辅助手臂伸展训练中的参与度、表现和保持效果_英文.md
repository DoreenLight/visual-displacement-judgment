# Personalized Adaptive Assistance With Reinforcement Learning Control Enhances Engagement, Performance, and Retention in Robot-Assisted Arm-Reaching Exercises

Andy Li , Graduate Student Member, IEEE, Riccardo Minto , Maximillan Dolling¨ , Giovanni Boschetti , and Damiano Zanotto , Member, IEEE

Abstract— This study introduces a new Reinforcement Learning Assist-as-Needed (RL-AAN) controller intended for robot-assisted upper-limb rehabilitation after stroke, which leverages a modified action-dependent heuristic dynamic programming (ADHDP) framework. Unlike conventional adaptive assist-as-needed controllers based on Iterative Learning Control (ILC-AAN), the proposed RL-AAN controller autonomously adjusts the trade-off between movement errors and robot assistance in response to the user’s recent performance, in real-time, while relying on a small set of high-level tunable parameters that do not require subject-specific manual adjustments. The RL-AAN controller was implemented on a cable-driven, end-effector type rehabilitation robot and validated against a conventional ILC-AAN controller through perturbationbased reaching tasks involving a group of healthy individuals. Compared to ILC-AAN, the RL-AAN controller significantly reduced the amount of robot assistance required during training, promoting user active participation and task performance. Following training with the RL-AAN controller, retention tests showed more accurate arm-reaching trajectories compared to ILC-AAN training, highlighting the potential of RL-AAN for future use in exercise-based rehabilitation. Overall, this work contributes to ongoing research into developing control strategies that enable personalization in physical human-robot interaction (pHRI) and robot-assisted rehabilitation.

Index Terms— Assist-as-needed control, reinforcement learning control, rehabilitation robotics, physical humanrobot interaction, cable-driven parallel robot.

## I. INTRODUCTION

R <sup>EHABILITATION</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>motor</sup> <sup>function</sup> <sup>is</sup> <sup>a</sup> <sup>fundamental</sup>component of the recovery process for individuals with component of the recovery process for individuals with movement impairments resulting from central or peripheral nervous system injuries (e.g., stroke, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury), neurodegenerative diseases, or musculoskeletal injuries [1]. Intense, repetitive, and task-oriented exercises can help individuals with brain injury regain lost motor functions through the process of motor (re)learning [2], [3]. However, traditional rehabilitative interventions are laborintensive and physically demanding for therapists and face constraints due to limited availability and the high cost of trained personnel, which restrict the frequency and intensity of exercise sessions. Robot-assisted rehabilitation aims to retrain movement planning, control, and execution through exercises mediated by a robotic trainer that delivers controlled assistive or resistive forces, often in conjunction with real-time multisensory feedback, and records quantitative data to inform targeted therapy. This technology has the potential to reduce the physical burden on therapists, optimize human resource allocation, and standardize rehabilitation programs [4], while also empowering patients to engage in self-administered, home-based exercises [5].

Because active efort is a critical enabler of motor learning [6], it has been postulated that a robotic trainer should only provide the minimum level of assistance required for the patient to accomplish a desired motor task. Thus, an ideal device should be transparent (i.e., exert no force on the user) when the user performs well, and intervene only when the user encounters dificulty completing the task, in order to correct excessive movement errors. Control strategies that align with this principle are referred to as “assist-as-needed” (AAN) [7], “patient-in-charge” [8], or “patient-cooperative” [9] controllers. Pioneering AAN strategies relied on conventional impedance controllers (IC) with fixed stifness and damping parameters, implementing a static relationship between movement errors and robot assistance [9], [10]. Such controllers require extensive manual tuning as the robot’s compliance must be adjusted to the patient’s varying motor skills over the therapy program [11]. To overcome this drawback, later works introduced adaptive AAN controllers that autonomously adjust the stifness of an underlying IC based on the patient’s most recent performance, leveraging a proportional iterative learning control (ILC) law [7], [12], [13]. Starting from a naive (untrained) state, an ILC-AAN controller updates the impedance parameter at each movement repetition by scaling the previous impedance parameter by a constant forgetting factor and adding a given function of the kinematic error measured at the previous repetition, multiplied by a constant gain. Other relevant examples of adaptive AAN controllers include: the error-based controller with an AAN force decay term introduced in [14], which modulates robot assistance by learning a model of the patient’s impairment across the robot’s workspace using radial basis functions; the joint-space controller developed in [15], which adjusts IC stifness based on human net joint torques estimated from on-board load cells via a linear adaptation map; and the optimal controller described in [16], where IC stifness is set inversely proportional to the estimated human joint stifness, to minimize the weighted sum of kinematic errors and robot’s efort. While all these approaches can gradually modulate the robot’s assistance as the user’s performances change, they share the following limitations: (i) they assume that the human motor system will always respond to robot-applied forces in the same stereotypical fashion, regardless of the therapy dosage already delivered–that is, the cumulative duration, intensity, and repetition frequency of prior training. This assumption fails to capture the non-linear, session-to-session learning dynamics observed during multi-session exercise, particularly in individuals with sensory or motor impairments; (ii) they are blind to previous movement executions, except for the most recent one; (iii) they operate with a fixed learning update rate, which must be manually adjusted to match the user’s learning pace at diferent stages of the rehabilitation program.

To address the limitations of conventional adaptive AAN controllers, more recent studies have aimed to modulate the level of assistance provided by a robotic trainer on-the-fly, using more advanced learning-based control architectures. Sharifi et al. developed a time-varying impedance controller with iterative learning-based adaptation laws to adjust the robot impedance based on human behavior [17]. This method seeks to optimize the balance between movement accuracy and stability, facilitating safe physical human-robot interaction without requiring force/torque measurements. However, it does not directly encourage user participation. Obayashi et al. proposed a task-oriented AAN controller to help users learn a dart-throwing task [18]. The AAN controller adopts a policygradient reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm to adjust the IC stifness based on the most recent throw score. However, this method does not modulate the relative importance of performance versus assistance as training progresses; thus both naive and experienced individuals are challenged using the same predefined control objective. Pareek et al. [19] proposed an RL–based AAN controller for handwriting rehabilitation, which was evaluated in a convenience sample of healthy individuals. This controller is trained ofline to derive a subject-independent policy that is not updated at runtime. As a result, the policy cannot autonomously adapt to inter-individual variability or session-to-session performance changes without retraining or manual parameter adjustment. Our group introduced the first stride-dependent [20], [21] and phase-dependent [22] RL-AAN controllers for robot assisted gait training, which autonomously reshape the IC of an ankle exoskeleton in real-time based on the wearer’s training performance. Subsequently, other researchers have proposed related RL-based strategies for lower-extremity exoskeletons [23], [24], [25].

Building upon the RL-AAN controller presented in [20] and [21], in this paper we leverage the vast expressive capability of actor-critic RL architectures to model the complex, subject-specific co-adaptation processes originating from the human-robot coupled system during repeated robot-assisted arm-reaching exercises. Although RL has been applied to functional electrical stimulation (FES) controllers for exercisebased rehabilitation and, more recently, to a variety of upperand lower-limb assistive and rehabilitation robots—including powered orthoses, prostheses, and end-efector-based devices [26]—work on RL-based AAN control remains limited. To the best of our knowledge, this paper introduces the first online RL-based AAN control strategy for rehabilitation robots explicitly designed to promote both movement accuracy and user active efort during upper-limb reaching exercises. From an algorithmic standpoint, the new RL-AAN controller extends previous research by explicitly accounting for the user active efort in the actor’s cost function, by modulating the balance between movement errors and robot assistance directly, through the actor’s control objective, and by autonomously varying the task complexity in response to the subject’s performance over recent movement repetitions following a performance-based curriculum learning scheme [27], [28], [29]. The resulting RL-AAN controller relies on a minimal set of subject-independent parameters, allowing for intuitive tuning and fast convergence. To demonstrate feasibility, we compared the efectiveness of the new RL-AAN controller to a conventional ILC-AAN controller, in terms of human motor adaptation during and immediately following sessions of robot-assisted arm-reaching tasks, which were administered using a custom-engineered cable-driven parallel robot (CDPR). The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section II introduces the experimental setup, including the CDPR setup and the implementation of robot-assisted arm-reaching exercises; Section III describes the proposed RL-AAN controller and the conventional ILC-AAN controller (used for comparison) which modulate the CDPR’s impedance during the armreaching exercises; Section IV deals with the experimental protocol used for validation with healthy individuals, and the statistical analysis. Results are presented in Section V and discussed in Section VI. Finally, conclusions and future work are presented in Section VII.

## A. Hardware Setup

The custom-engineered CDPR, shown in Fig. 1, uses four coated steel cables arranged in parallel to exert desired planar forces on its end-efector (joystick). The joystick can be moved by the user over a planar workspace $( 6 3 0 \times 6 3 0 \mathrm { m m } ^ { 2 } )$ ), similar to a computer mouse. Each cable is controlled by a spool actuated by a brushed DC motor (Groschopp PM6015, 57 W, 220 mNm rated torque) and equipped with an incremental encoder (US Digital E5-1000, 4000 CPR). An idle pulley mounted at the tip of a cantilever beam loadcell measures the tensile force in each cable and routes the cable towards a pair of vertically mounted idle rollers. Each pair of idle rollers is located at one vertex of the square workspace, serving as the efective cable exit points for the CDPR. The distal end of each cable is connected to the same point on the vertical axis of the joystick. The top part of the joystick can rotate freely about the same vertical axis to minimize the transmission of spurious moments during motion. An acrylic plate covering the CDPR workspace and a low-friction adhesive film attached to the bottom surface of the joystick minimize the efect of sliding friction. A Real-Time Target Machine (SpeedGoat Baseline) operating at 1kHz serves as the CDPR controller and handles communication with the motor drivers (A-M-C DZEANTU-020B200) and a custom-engineered data acquisition (DAQ) board via an EtherCAT fieldbus. A 2D virtual playground developed in Unity provides the user interface for the armreaching exercises. Task-relevant data are sent from the CDPR controller to the Unity environment at 60 Hz through a User Datagram Protocol (UDP) socket.

## B. Robot-Assisted Arm-Reaching Exercise

A serious game was implemented in the CDPR to challenge the user’s ability to counter an external perturbation force during arm-reaching tasks. Starting from the center of the workspace, the user moves the joystick along a straight-line path displayed on a computer screen, as accurately as possible, while being subjected to the perturbation, to reach one of six predefined targets arranged at the vertices of a regular hexagon, located 20 cm from the starting position (Fig. 2-a). The user then brings the joystick back to the starting position and waits for the next target to appear at a random vertex. To pace the task, a blue indicator progresses from the center of the hexagon towards the current target within 1 second, following a $5 ^ { \mathrm { t h } } .$ -order time law [34]. A 500 ms pause is inserted between the appearance of the next target on the screen, and the onset of the blue indicator motion. The color of the reference path is modulated by the current kinematic error $d ,$ serving as descriptive visual feedback. When the error is small $( d \digamma <$ 2 5 mm), the color is set to green. When the error is moderate $( 2 . 5 \mathrm { m m } \leq d < 7 . 5 \mathrm { m m } )$ , the color progressively shifts from green to yellow, and when the error is high $( d \ge 7 . 5 $ mm), it gradually transitions to red. The external perturbation $F _ { p e r t }$ is defined as

$$
F _ {p e r t} = \gamma_ {p e r t} \mathbf {v} _ {\perp},\tag{1}
$$

Thus, the perturbation peaks when the upper limb moves the fastest. This perturbation strategy has been validated in prior studies as an efective way to introduce disturbance in the user’s arm movement while ensuring safety and avoiding fatigue [35]. $$

## III. ASSIST-AS-NEEDED CONTROL

In this work, we investigate the modulation of the impedance parameter g in (2) by an adaptive AAN controller. We compared a novel RL-AAN controller against a traditional ILC-AAN controller and hypothesized that the enhanced versatility in adapting to user performances aforded by the learning-based method would result in improved human motor adaptation dynamics during the exercise and better retention after training with the assistive force. For both controllers the impedance parameter g was allowed to vary within the interval [0<sub>,</sub> 0<sub>.</sub>5] mm<sup>−1</sup> (Fig. 2-b).

## A. ILC-AAN Controller

ILC is often utilized in robotic applications involving repetitive tasks. ILC aims to improve task execution by gradually adjusting the control action after each task repetition, leveraging feedback from previous repetitions [12]. In the present implementation, a P-type ILC-AAN controller [7], [37] adjusts the impedance parameter g to be applied during the next movement repetition (hereafter referred to as “cycle”) according to

$$
g _ {k + 1} = \gamma_ {I L C} g _ {k} + f (d _ {k}),\tag{4a}
$$

$$
f (d _ {k}) = K d _ {k} - b,\tag{4b}
$$

where k is the index of the most recently completed cycle, $f ( d )$ is a linear function of the adjusted movement error $d ,$ with gain $K > 0$ and intercept $^ { b , }$ and $\gamma _ { I L C } \in ( 0 , 1 )$ is the forgetting factor. Appropriate tuning of $\gamma _ { I L C }$ , K, and b is critical to ensure adaptability to user performance and promote active participation. The forgetting term $\gamma _ { I L C }$ must be adjusted to prevent the user from over-relying on the CDPR assistance, thereby reducing their own efort [38]. Indeed, $\gamma _ { I L C }$ determines the rate at which the learning algorithm reduces $g$ when the movement error is small, steering the CDPR towards a weaker assistive force field (2), with the efect of challenging the user. This fading assistance has positive efects in terms of motor learning [39]. If K is not properly tuned in relation to $\gamma _ { I L C } ,$ the controller may not be able to compensate for the efect of the forgetting factor. This could decrease g even when the error is substantial, or, conversely, saturate $g$ even at small errors, efectively leading to a static IC. In this work, we selected the ILC parameters as shown in Tab. I, based on preliminary tests.

Despite its popularity, ILC-AAN requires extensive trialand-error tuning, and its myopic control law (4), which accounts only for the user’s most recent performance, may fail to efectively replicate the complex dynamics of human motor adaptation. This motivates the development of a new learning-based AAN controller, as described in Sec. III-B.

## B. RL-AAN Controller

Unlike other optimal control strategies, RL controllers learn an optimal policy on-the-fly, while interacting with the environment [40]. This is especially convenient when an accurate model of the environment is dificult to obtain, as is the case of physical human-robot interaction (pHRI) involving complex human-robot dynamics. Herein, we relied on the actor-critic RL framework [41], whereby the actor determines the most suitable action at each cycle k, based on the system states and feedback from the critic, while the critic assesses the efectiveness of that action in terms of the anticipated longterm costs. In the context of RL control, the long-term cost at the k-th cycle, denoted as V(k), is defined as the discounted sum of all future negative rewards [42]

$$
V (k) = \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {\infty} \gamma_ {R L} ^ {i} r (k + i + 1),\tag{5}
$$

where $r ( k )$ represents the immediate negative reward incurred at the k-th cycle, and $\gamma _ { R L } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ is the discount factor. The basic idea behind Heuristic Dynamic Programming (HDP) is to estimate V(k) via function approximation [43]. Following this approach, the critic neural network (NN) estimates the approximated long-term cost $\tilde { V } ( k )$ at the k-th cycle by minimizing the temporal diference error $t _ { d } ( k )$ derived from the Bellman equation [44]:

$$
t _ {d} (k) = r (k) + \gamma_ {R L} \tilde {V} (k) - \tilde {V} (k - 1)\tag{6}
$$

In this work, we adopted the Action-Dependant HDP (ADHDP) [40], a modified HDP algorithm that is well suited for complex systems and features convenient uniformly ultimate boundedness (UUB) properties [45]. Following the ADHDP method, the critic NN estimates $\tilde { V } ( k )$ as a stateaction-value function, considering both the states and the action as inputs (Fig. 3). Thus, the optimal policy is the one minimizing $\tilde { V } ( k )$ . In the present implementation, the RL-AAN model is characterized by:

• States $\begin{array} { r c l } { S ( k ) } & { = } & { [ \bar { e } _ { p o s } ( k ) , \bar { e } _ { n e g } ( k ) , e _ { p o s } ^ { + } ( k ) , e _ { n e g } ^ { + } ( k ) ] } \end{array}$ , where $\bar { e } _ { p o s } ( k ) , e _ { p o s } ^ { + } ( k )$ and $\bar { e } _ { n e g } ( k ) , e _ { n e g } ^ { + } ( k )$ are the mean (indicated by the overline) and peak (indicated by the + superscript) positive and negative errors measured during the k-th cycle. Positive errors are those in the same direction of the perturbation force, $F _ { p e r t }$ . Negative errors are those opposite to $F _ { p e r t }$ . Thus, positive and negative errors indicate under-correction and over-correction by the user, respectively.

• A set of possible actions $g ( k ) \in [ 0 , 0 . 5 ] ~ \mathrm { m m ^ { - 1 } }$

• An immediate cost $r ( k ) = S ^ { T } ( k ) \Lambda S ( k )$ , where $\Lambda \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 4 \times 4 }$ is a positive-definite diagonal weight matrix.

Both the actor and the critic networks are nonlinear feedforward NNs with one hidden layer and sigmoid activation functions [40]. The critic NN is parameterized as

$$
\tilde {V} (k) = W _ {c} ^ {(2)} (k) \sigma (H _ {c} (k)),\tag{7a}
$$

$$
H _ {c} (k) = W _ {c} ^ {(1)} (k) \left[ S ^ {T} (k), \delta_ {a} (k) \right] ^ {T},\tag{7b}
$$

where $W _ { c } ^ { ( 1 ) } ( k )$ and $W _ { c } ^ { ( 2 ) } ( k )$ are the weights matrices for the hidden and output layers, respectively, $H _ { c } ( k )$ is the hidden layer that connects $\bar { W } _ { c } ^ { ( 1 ) } ( k )$ and $W _ { c } ^ { ( 2 ) } ( k )$ , and $\delta _ { a } ( k )$ is the output of the actor NN. The gradient descent method updates the critic NN weights, with a learning rate $\alpha _ { c } .$ , to minimize the temporal diference error (6) according to the quadratic cost $E _ { c } ( k )$

$$
E _ {c} (k) = \frac {1}{2} t _ {d} ^ {2} (k)\tag{8}
$$

The weights of the actor NN are updated using the gradient descent method with learning rate $\alpha _ { a }$ to minimize the actor cost function $E _ { a } ( k )$ , defined as

$$
E _ {a} (k) = \frac {1}{2} \left[ \tilde {V} (k) (1 - U _ {c} (k)) + g (k) U _ {c} (k) \right] ^ {2},\tag{9}
$$

where $U _ { c } ( k )$ is a specified control objective. Because the actor output $\delta _ { a } ( k )$ is bounded to the interval [0 1] by the sigmoid activation function, a scaling factor $\rho = 0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m m ^ { - 1 } }$ is introduced to map the impedance parameter to the feasible interval [0 0 5] mm<sup>−1</sup>:

$$
g (k) = \rho \delta_ {a} (k)\tag{10}
$$

The control objective $U _ { c } ( k )$ in (9) is updated after each cycle based on the user’s performance over the last $w _ { U }$ cycles, through the following law

$$
U _ {c} (k + 1) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \min \left(1,   U _ {c} (k) + \beta\right) & i f \overline {{r}} (k) \leq \eta (k) \\ \max \left(0,   U _ {c} (k) - \beta\right) & i f \overline {{r}} (k) > \eta (k), \end{array} \right.\tag{11}
$$

where ${ \overline { { r } } } ( k )$ is the arithmetic mean of the immediate costs measured over the last w cycles, $\beta$ is the rate of change of $U _ { c } ( k )$ , and $\eta ( k )$ indicates the current level of acceptable error. We note that $U _ { c } ( k )$ is constrained to the interval [0 1] by definition. Thus, the actor cost (9) is the squared convex combination of $\tilde { V } ( k )$ , representing combined kinematic errors, and $g ( k )$ , indicating the current level of robot assistance, with their relative importance modulated by $U _ { c } ( k )$ . In essence, $U _ { c }$ controls the balance between two conflicting objectives in exercise training: minimizing external assistance to encourage active efort, vs. minimizing movement errors to promote correct task execution. When the user performs well, the controller increases $U _ { c } ,$ , thereby accepting larger kinematic errors and further penalizing robot guidance, which results in a more compliant joystick. Conversely, when movement errors increase, the controller decreases $U _ { c }$ , stepping up the assistive action to reduce kinematic errors.

As can be inferred from (11), a sustained value of $U _ { c } ( k ) = 1$ indicates consistently acceptable performance, whereas a consistently low $U _ { c } ( k ) < 1$ in recent executions suggests that the task has become too challenging for the user. Leveraging this insight, we update the level of acceptable error η(k) based on the user performance measured over the last $w _ { \eta }$ cycles, according to the following law:

$$
\eta (k + 1) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \max \left(\eta_ {\text { low }},   \eta (k) - \zeta\right) \text { if } U _ {c} (k - i) = 1   \forall   i \in [ 0, w _ {\eta} ] \\ \min \left(\eta_ {\text { high }},   \eta (k) + \zeta\right) \text { if } \nexists i \in [ 0, w _ {\eta} ] \text { s.t. } U _ {c} (k - i) = 1 \\ \eta (k) \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \text { otherwise } \end{array} \right.\tag{12}
$$

As indicated in (12), if all $U _ { c }$ values within the assessing window $w _ { \eta }$ are unitary, a more challenging objective is set by decreasing η by a fixed quantity ζ. However, if the user never reaches a unitary $U _ { c }$ in the last $w _ { \eta }$ cycles, η is increased by the same quantity to reduce task dificulty. Based on preliminary tests, we determined that $\eta _ { l o w } \ = \ 5$ mm and $\eta _ { h i g h } \ = \ 2$ mm were perceived as low and high-dificulty levels, respectively. Thus, by setting ζ = 1 5 mm, the controller can switch across three equally-spaced levels of dificulty: $\eta _ { l o w } , \eta _ { m e d } , \eta _ { h i g h } .$ Additionally $w _ { \eta }$ was set to increase as η drops $( w _ { \eta , l o w } = 6 ,$ $w _ { \eta , m e d } ~ = ~ 1 2 , ~ w _ { \eta , h i g h } ~ = ~ 3 0 )$ to encourage users to explore more challenging motor tasks. ## IV. EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION

## A. Protocol

Fifteen healthy adults (11 males, age 18-32 years) volunteered for this study. Participants had no musculoskeletal or neurological injuries that would afect their ability to complete reaching tasks with their dominant arm. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Stevens Institute of Technology (Protocol 2022-034), and all participants provided written consent prior to data collection. The study included two visits, one for each controller (ILC-AAN and RL-AAN), scheduled at least 48 hours apart to ensure a suficient washout period [46]. To account for any potential residual carry-over efects not fully eliminated by the washout period, the order of the controllers was balanced across participants using a Latin square design.

The protocol followed in each visit is illustrated in Fig. 4. First, each participant underwent a 60-cycle familiarization session (FAM), consisting of 30 arm-reaching repetitions without $F _ { p e r t } ,$ and 30 repetitions with $F _ { p e r t }$ , spanning all six directions, in random order. This session was carried out with null $F _ { A A N }$ to let the participant familiarize themselves with the task and the type of perturbation generated by the CDPR. Next, participants completed a baseline session (BSLN) consisting of 60 arm-reaching cycles (10 along each direction, in random sequence) performed with null $F _ { A A N }$ and $F _ { p e r t } ,$ to evaluate their unperturbed/unassisted task performance. Subsequently, participants underwent a 60-cycle pre-training (PRE) session, during which they were exposed to $F _ { p e r t }$ without receiving external assistance. The purpose of this session was to evaluate how each participant responded to unexpected disturbances. Data collected from this session was also compared to posttraining data to evaluate any training efects. Additionally, for the RL-AAN controller, the PRE session was used to pre-train the critic NN, starting from a naive state and setting g ≡ 0 in place of the actor output. This approach improves data eficiency by allowing the critic NN to observe the environment under a transparent policy [19]. Afterwards, participants took part in 4 training sessions (T-1 to T-4), each consisting of 180 cycles (i.e., 30 cycles along each direction, in random order) with both $F _ { p e r t }$ and $F _ { A A N }$ active. The RL-AAN and ILC-AAN controllers adapted cycle-by-cycle throughout the entire training period (T-1 to T-4) without being reset. Fiveminute breaks were included in-between PRE and T-1 to T-4 sessions to minimize physical and mental fatigue. To assess the development of internal feed-forward models in the study participants, 12 catch trials – evenly distributed across the 6 movement directions – were administered during each training session. Specifically, one catch trial was randomly inserted into each 30-cycle block by unexpectedly removing both $F _ { A A N }$ and $F _ { p e r t } ,$ as described in [47]. These 12 catch trials were conducted in addition to the 180 cycles that comprised each training session. Finally, training efects were assessed during three post-training sessions (PT1 to PT3), each including 60 cycles with $F _ { p e r t }$ but no assistance. These sessions began 1, 10, and 20 minutes after the end of T-4 (Fig. 4), respectively, in line with previous studies [22], [48].

## B. Statistical Analysis

To compare the efectiveness of the two controllers in promoting motor adaptations during training, a linear mixed model (LMM) analysis was carried out with Controller (ILC-AAN, RL-AAN) and Session (T-1 to T-4) as independent variables. Intercept, Session, Controller, and Session × Controller were set as fixed efects, and Intercept was also set as a random efect, to account for individual diferences in initial performance:

$$
Y _ {i j} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} S _ {i j} + \beta_ {2} C _ {i j} + \beta_ {3} S _ {i j} C _ {i j} + u _ {0 i} + \varepsilon_ {i j}\tag{13}
$$

In the above equation, i and j are the indices of the study participant and training session, respectively; $S _ { i j } \in \{ 0 , 1 , 2 , 3 \}$ and $C _ { i j } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ encode Session and Controller, respectively; u is the subject’s random Intercept, and $\varepsilon _ { i j } \sim \mathcal { N } ( 0 , \sigma ^ { 2 } )$ represents residuals [49]. The dependent variables included the average impedance parameter g, evaluated over each T session and used as a surrogate measure for robot assistance, and the percent changes from PRE to each T session in the mean positive and negative errors $( \Delta \bar { e } _ { p o s } \mathcal { I } _ { o } , \ \Delta \bar { e } _ { n e g } \mathcal { I } _ { o } )$ , which quantified changes in an individual’s tendency toward undercorrection and over-correction. To assess whether there was a decline in $\eta$ over the training sessions, indicating performance improvement when using the RL-AAN controller, we conducted an LMM analysis on the total number of cycles executed at each η. The model included Movement Cycle as a fixed efect, and Intercept as both a fixed and random efect:

$$
Y _ {i j} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} M _ {i j} + u _ {0 i} + \varepsilon_ {i j}\tag{14}
$$

In the above equation, $M _ { i j }$ encodes the Movement Cycle, while all other parameters are defined as in (13). As a secondary analysis, catch trials were examined using one sample t-tests applied to the diferences in mean negative errors between each catch trial and the cycle immediately preceding it.

Next, to establish the presence of learning efects, we carried out a third LMM analysis, similar to (13), using Intercept, Session (PT1 to PT3), Controller, and Session × Controller as fixed efects, Intercept as a random efect, and percent diferences in the error metrics between PRE and each PT session $( \Delta \bar { e } _ { p o s } \mathcal { I } _ { o } , \ \Delta \bar { e } _ { n e g } \mathcal { I } _ { o } )$ as dependent variables. This analysis evaluated subjects’ short-term retention, defined as their ability to track the target motion while compensating for the perturbation in the absence of robotic assistance.

The assumption of normality was assessed by inspecting QQ-plots of the residuals (for LMM analyses) and by applying the Shapiro-Wilk test (for one-sample t-tests). Potential correlations between $\Delta \bar { e } _ { p o s } \%$ and $\Delta \bar { e } _ { n e g } \%$ , were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coeficients (ρ). A diagonal covariance structure was selected for the LMM analyses to account for potential deviations from homoskedasticity. All samples (cycles), except those from the catch trials, were used to compute the subject- and session-specific averages for the LMM analyses. For the catch-trial analysis, each subject’s responses across the six directions were pooled to obtain a subject-specific mean value, which was then used in the one-sample tests. The significance level was set to $\alpha ~ = ~ . 0 5$ , and all data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0.

## B. Retention

The LMM analysis of the post-training data revealed a significant reduction in undercompensation (i.e., a positive $\Delta \bar { e } _ { p o s } \%$ intercept) relative to PRE performance immediately following training with RL-AAN $( p ~ < ~ . 0 0 1 )$ , which was retained across all three PT sessions without an appreciable decline in performance (Tab. II). Although a significant decrease in undercompensation was also observed immediately after ILC-AAN training $( p ~ < ~ . 0 5 )$ , this improvement was less pronounced than that observed after RL-AAN training $( p < . 0 0 1 )$ , indicating more accurate task execution following RL-AAN traininig. This was likely due to participants overrelying on the ILC assistance during training. Indeed, PT1 started just 1 minute after the end of T-4. As the stif ILC assistance was suddenly removed, subjects had to readapt to efectively counteract $F _ { p e r t }$ without robot guidance. The RL-AAN method generated a more compliant assistance throughout the training (Fig. 7a), thus mitigating this undesirable after efect. In summary, the higher exposure to the perturbation during training with the RL-AAN controller elicited more accurate retention of the target movement following training, compared to the ILC-AAN controller, the gains in performance being largely driven by a lower tendency to undercompensate $F _ { p e r t }$

## VI. DISCUSSION

In recent years, there has been increasing attention on rehabilitation technologies that enable the individualization of therapy procedures [50]. Traditionally, generalized control approaches have been deployed with limited success [38]; however, acknowledging the variability in individuals behavioral profiles has unveiled new possibilities for robotassisted treatment [51], [52], [53]. Robotic technologies are well-positioned to continuously evaluate an individual’s motor performance and adjust exercise challenge levels accordingly, thereby promoting sustained engagement [54]. Integrating personalization in robot-assisted exercise training may facilitate the development of internal models, enhancing the efectiveness of rehabilitation therapies [55], [56].

This study introduced a new RL-based AAN controller for robot-assisted arm-reaching exercises, demonstrating that the new RL-AAN controller outperforms a conventional ILCbased adaptive controller in achieving the primary objective of AAN control: to self-adapt the level of assistance provided by a rehabilitation robot to an individual’s performance, ofering only the minimal assistance needed for them to complete the motor task, to encourage active participation and promote motor adaptation. The conventional ILC-AAN controller relies on a simplistic law that only considers the most recent movement execution and always responds in the same standardized way to the user’s motor behaviors, irrespective of the amount of training received. Conversely, the proposed RL-AAN controller encourages active participation at three diferent levels. First, unlike ILC-AAN, RL-AAN considers multiple error components in its states. By distinguishing overcompensation $( \bar { e } _ { n e g } )$ from undercompensation $( \bar { e } _ { p o s } )$ , it can track user engagement after each movement execution and more efectively identify and exploit hidden patterns relating user participation to long-term performance. Second, the actor NN continuously seeks an optimal policy minimizing a varying cost that embeds both measures of an individual’s engagement (physical efort) and estimates of their future motor behaviors (kinematic errors), leveraging the user’s performance history to modulate the relative importance between the two contributions via the control objective $U _ { c } .$ . Finally, the RL model features a performance-based curriculum learning structure [27], [28], [29], which adjusts the level of task dificulty by dynamically modifying the level of acceptable error η [21], thereby allowing the RL-AAN controller to not only adjust the assistance level but also to scale task dificulty based on user performance. This curriculum-learning-based adaptation mechanism promotes sustained engagement and may facilitate motor learning by keeping the task within the user’s zone of proximal development, i.e., the space between what a learner can do without assistance and what they can do with assistance [57]. Overall, the LMM analysis indicated that the RL-AAN controller induced an increasing trend in user engagement during training, resulting in better retention after training compared to the conventional ILC-AAN controller.

Two studies on RL-based controllers for robot-assisted upper-limb rehabilitation incorporated minimization of robotic assistance into their model design [18], [19]. While both studies reported improvements in error reduction, they did not include a quantitative analysis of user engagement during training and retention sessions, nor did they administer catch trials to validate the establishment of internal models. In this context, the findings of the present study provide new insights, supporting our initial hypothesis that the enhanced adaptability of the RL-AAN controller may lead to more eficient learning compared to the conventional ILC-AAN method, as evidenced by improved human motor adaptation dynamics during the exercise and more accurate retention following training.

Due to its limited adaptability, the ILC-AAN controller is intrinsically dificult to fine-tune to an individual. The optimal forgetting factor and error gain can vary significantly across subjects, and must be determined by trial-and-error. This is an extremely tedious task, as it requires multiple rounds of testing and validation, with suficient washout periods inbetween trials. For this reason, most studies on the topic have adopted one-size-fits-all settings [7], [13]. In contrast, the proposed RL-AAN controller achieves personalization, without requiring individualized tuning, by incorporating the control objective $U _ { c }$ and the high-level performance parameter $\eta .$ This approach accounts for user engagement and simplifies the model’s tuning process. This architecture provides a leveled structure ensuring that both the actor and critic NNs converge rapidly, significantly enhancing the model’s realtime adaptability to individual behaviors, and eliminating the need for manual tuning. Furthermore, $U _ { c }$ and $\eta$ facilitate more intuitive performance monitoring and system adjustments.

This simplified tuning process ofers an advantage not only over traditional ILC-based controllers, but also over more recent learning-based models. While RL-based algorithms generally require less parameters to be manually tuned compared to traditional ILC-based controllers, they also necessitate large pre-training datasets [18], [58], making them less practical for real-world applications. For example, the AR3n controller proposed in [19] requires a considerable amount of pre-training data and a simulated training environment, which adds another layer of complexity. In contrast, the proposed RL-AAN controller takes advantage of a curriculum-learningbased design that does not require any large pre-trained dataset. The critic NN converges during the PRE session, significantly lowering the demand of data and mitigating participant burden. In summary, through continuous re-balancing of kinematic error and robotic efort via the actor cost $U _ { c } ,$ the proposed RL-AAN controller achieves user adaptation beyond what is aforded by traditional ILC laws and ofline RL methods.

The present study also highlighted several limitations of the proposed RL-AAN framework. First, the acceptable error thresholds $( \eta _ { l o w } , \eta _ { m e d } ,$ and $\eta _ { h i g h } )$ were manually set to uniform values across all participants, and the network learning rates $( \alpha _ { c } , \ \alpha _ { a } )$ were defined from pilot data. This approach introduces subjectivity and may not generalize well to users with heterogeneous functional impairments. Developing automated methods to tune these thresholds and adapt learning rates based on individual performance would enhance both objectivity and generalizability. Second, future work should include a sensitivity analysis to examine the relative impact of the RL-AAN parameters in Tab. I in terms of user performance. In terms of experimental design, incorporating a control condition with visual feedback but no physical guidance would be valuable for elucidating how RL- and ILC-based assistance compare to unassisted practice.

While this study suggests the efectiveness of the RL-AAN algorithm in inducing short-term motor adaptations in healthy individuals, additional work is required before this approach can be piloted in individuals post-stroke. Stroke survivors often exhibit hemiparetic weakness, spasticity, abnormal muscle synergies, reduced shoulder and elbow range of motion, and sensory deficits —factors that can profoundly influence reaching task performance [59]. Notably, substantial directiondependent diferences in transverse-plane reaching have been reported in this population. Diagonal movements toward the contralateral-front quadrant and back toward the ipsilateralback quadrant appear the most sensitive to impairment level [60], likely due to common deficits in elbow-shoulder coordination (e.g., flexion synergy). By contrast, findings for anteroposterior and mediolateral reaching tasks have been mixed [61], [62]. Therefore, future work involving stroke survivors should incorporate larger samples and increased repetitions per target to examine whether movement direction moderates performance during and after RL-AAN training.

Two recent meta-analyses of randomized clinical trials on robot-assisted upper-limb therapy post-stroke found significant immediate benefits of robotic therapy over standard care when total training time exceeded 15 hours, the robot operated in a “patient-active” mode (i.e., patient-cooperative, AAN, IC, or EMG-based control), and therapy was delivered to firsttime stroke patients with mild to moderate impairment [63], [64]. However, both studies found no evidence of superiority of robotic over conventional therapy in follow-up assessments. Therefore, a future pilot cross-sectional study evaluating safety and proof-of-concept clinical feasibility of the RL-AAN controller should be followed by a longitudinal trial incorporating extended (≥ 15 hrs.) robotic training and follow-up evaluations to determine whether the benefits of adaptive and personalized AAN robotic therapy are sustained over time. Furthermore, since rehabilitation gains are largely task-specific [2], further research is warranted to explore the application of the presented RL-AAN strategy to robotic platforms that enable reaching tasks beyond the transverse-plane motions investigated in this paper, as well as those enabling training of the distal upper limb [64].

## VII. CONCLUSION

This work introduced the first on-line RL-based assist-asneeded strategy explicitly designed to promote both movement accuracy and user active efort during upper-limb reaching exercises. The proposed RL-AAN controller employs an ADHDP framework to modulate the level of robotic assistance based on the user’s recent performance. Unlike other RL-based controllers for upper-limb rehabilitation, the proposed controller dynamically adjusts the balance between user performance and efort in the actor cost, as well as the task dificulty, to encourage active participation and promote motor adaptation.

The RL-AAN controller was validated against a conventional ILC-AAN controller through tests with healthy individuals using a cable-driven rehabilitation robot. During training, the RL-AAN controller outperformed the ILC-AAN controller by eliciting similar levels of movement accuracy on the user while applying substantially lower assistance. Furthermore, study participants demonstrated more accurate retention of the target movement after training with the RL-AAN controller.

This work contributes to ongoing research demonstrating the benefits of personalization in pHRI and robot-assisted training, paving the way for future validation studies with brain-injured populations.
