## Abstract

Exoskeletons are increasingly used in rehabilitation and daily life in persons with motor disorders after neurological injuries. The overall objective of this thesis is to study how to robustly and accurately predict joint torque using inputs from sensors that would technically be feasible to equip on an assistive exoskeleton, and to develop a framework that could be used to evaluate the user-exoskeleton interface. This compilation thesis is based on five papers that focus on diferent aspects of exoskeleton controller design and physical design, to contribute to the design of wearable exoskeleton systems for rehabilitation and movement assistance.

In the first study, we estimated ankle joint torques using an electromyography (EMG)-driven neuromusculoskeletal (NMS) model and a standard artificia neural network (ANN) model in seven movement tasks. EMG signals and ankle joint angles were used as input in each method, the models were trained with experimental data from 3D motion analysis, and ankle joint torques were predicted. Our results suggested that the standard-ANN model could predict ankle torques more accurately when trained on a large and varied set of trials but less accurately in unseen movements whereas the NMS model was overal more useful and has the advantage of detailing underlying physical principles. The second study was directly inspired by the first study. Our aim was to estimate ankle joint torques using a hybrid form of the two methods in the first study, specifically an NMS solver-informed ANN, by augmenting a standard-ANN model with additional features from the underlying NMS solver. The torque prediction performance of hybrid-ANN models was investigated in two scenarios: intra-subject and inter-subject tests. We found that the NMS solver informed-ANN model had a better joint torque prediction accuracy than the methods independently (i.e. NMS or the standard-ANN model), which indicates that benefit was gained from integrating NMS features into standard ANN models. Furthermore, in the inter-subject tests, we adopted the transfer learning technique to further improve the joint torque performance by taking advantage of the information extracted from previous subjects.

In the third study, we predicted not only ankle joint torques, but also knee joint torques in the sagittal plane and hip joint torques in both sagittal and frontal planes during several diferent motions using long short-term memory memory (LSTM) neural networks. Several networks were constructed, namely separate LSTM models for each movement as well as one uniform model for all movements. The LSTM networks were constructed both with and without transfer learning, in which the networks could take advantage of information extracted from previous tasks and/or subjects to predict for an unseen or “new” task or subject. We evaluated the LSTM models’ prediction ability across tasks and subjects, and studied whether generalizability was improved when transfer learning was implemented in the LSTM networks. Our results indicate that both one uniform and ten separate LSTM models predicted lower limb joint torques accurately, i.e. with low prediction error, in intra-subject tests. By including transfer learning, the LSTM models’ generalizability in predicting moments across tasks and subjects was significantly improved, even if re-trained with a smaller subset of movements from the “new” subject.

In the fourth and fifth studies, we generated a realistic knee exoskeleton model. In the fourth study, we created a virtual human-machine system (HMS) in a musculoskeletal modeling software. We found that this proposed virtua HMS was useful for simulating how diferent assistive strategies may afect parameters that describe the demands and consequences on the user, including muscular demand, joint contact forces and human-machine interactive forces involved in movements. In the fifth study, we analyzed, through simulation, how diferent weight distributions of knee exoskeleton components influenced the user’s muscle activation during three functional movements. One unilatera knee exoskeleton prototype was fabricated and tested on five healthy subjects. Simulation and experimental muscle activations were compared. We found that muscle activation varied among weight distributions and movements, indicating that no single physical design was optimal for all movements. Exoskeleton physical design should ideally consider the user’s activity goals.

This methods developed in this thesis hold great promise in applications such as the design of assistance-as-needed exoskeleton control. The developed virtua HMS makes it possible to study how a knee exoskeleton’s diferent assistive strategies and physical designs can influence its user’s muscle efort and interaction forces, which can both simplify prototype iterations and evaluate some parameters that are otherwise dificult to measure experimentally.

Keywords: Hill-type muscle model, generalization, transfer learning, computationally modeling, human-machine system

Exoskelett används alltmer vid rehabilitering och dagligt liv hos personer med motoriska störningar efter neurologiska skador. Det övergripande syftet med denna avhandling är att skapa robusta och noggranna metoder för att förutsäga vridmomentet i nedre extremiteterna med bärbara sensorer samt för att utvärdera i en simulering krafterna i gränssnittet mellan ett exoskelett och en användare. Denna avhandling grundar sig på fem studier var fokus är på olika aspekter av en exoskeletts reglerteknik och fysisk design, i en kontext för tillämpning i framtagning av exoskelett för rehabilitering och rörelseassistans.

I den första studien har vi uppskattat vridmomentet i fotleden i sju olika rörelser med hjälp av en elektromyografi (EMG) driven neuromuskuloskeletal (NMS) modell och en standard artificiell neuralt nätverk (ANN) modell. Våra resultat tyder på att ANN modellen kunde uppskatta vridmoment noggrannare än NMS modellen om den tränades på mycket data från många typer av rörelser; men NMS modellen uppskattade noggrannare när den tränades med data från liknande rörelser. Dessutom har NMS modellen fördelen av att kunna beskriva underliggande fysiska principer. I den tredje studien uppskattade vi vridmoment i fotleden och i knäleden i sagittalplan, samt i höftleden i både sagittal- och frontalplan, under flera olika rörelser med hjälp av ett LSTM neuralt nätverk. Flera nätverk konstruerades, nämligen separata LSTM modeller för varje rörelse samt en enhetlig modell för alla rörelser. LSTM nätverken konstruerades både med och utan s.k. “transfer learning”, där ett nätverk kan dra nytta av kunskap från tidigare rörelser och/eller försökspersoner för att bättre uppskatta vridmoment hos en tidigare okänd eller “ ny ” rörelse eller testperson. Vi utvärderade hur noggrant som LSTM modellerna kunde uppskatta vridmoment när de tränades och testades på olika rörelser och försökspersoner. En av studiefrågorna var huruvida modellernas generaliserbarhet förbättrades när transfer learning implementerades.

Våra resultat tyder på att både den enhetliga och de tio individuella LSTM modellerna kunde noggrant uppskattade vridmoment. Transfer learning ytterli gare ökade deras generaliserbarhet för nya rörelser och försökspersoner, även om dataset för träning från den “nya” testpersonen innehöll mindre data från färre rörelser.

I den fjärde och femte studien skapade vi en realistisk knäexoskeletmodell. I den fjärde studien skapade vi ett virtuellt human-machine system (HMS) i biomekanikmodellering mjukvara. En knäexoskelett prototyp tillverkades och testades på fem friska försökspersoner. Exoskelettets påverkan på användarens muskelaktiveringen jämfördes i både simuleringen och i experimenten. Vi fann att muskelaktivering varierade mellan viktfördelningar och rörelser, d.v.s. ingen enstaka fysisk design var optimal för alla rörelser. Den fysiska designen för ett exoskeletett bör helst beakta användarens aktivitetsmål.

## Chapter 1

# Introduction

Human gaits are a repetitive sequence of limb motion that move the body forward while maintaining stance stability (Perry 1992). Neurological diseases, such as stroke, spinal cord injury, and brain trauma, often impair one’s ability and strategy to walk. In recent decades, wearable exoskeletons have been developed to restore or augment motor function and/or facilitate neuro-rehabilitation. Wearable exoskeletons are promising assistive/rehabilitative devices that can help enhance motor capacity in people with strength deficits or motor disorders. The scope of the thesis is about biomechanical parameter estimation for wearable exoskeleton system design that can contribute to rehabilitation and movement assistance.

During exoskeleton-assisted rehabilitation training, patients’ active participation is essential for stimulating neuromuscular recovery. However, despite recent developments in mechatronics and biosignal processing, exoskeleton contro algorithms that involve the active participation of neurologically afected patients are still few. Therefore mechanistic understanding of how muscles activate to produce force and joint torque to propel the body toward a specific motion would be beneficial in the control strategy of an exoskeleton for neuro-rehabilitation, as it can guide that control to encompass and even encourage patients’ active participation (Mounis et al. 2019).

While experimental evaluation of exoskeleton prototypes is important, biomechanical predictive simulations early in the design process can minimize prototype iterations and evaluate some parameters that are otherwise dificult to measure experimentally. Virtual human-machine systems (HMSs) have been used numerous times in optimal design and control of exoskeletons. However, few virtual HMSs can provide detailed impact on users at joint and muscle levels as well as interaction forces on diferent assistive strategies and weight distributions of exoskeletons before experiments (Shourijeh et al. 2017; Dembia et al. 2017).

## 1.1. Biomechanics of normal gait

When a person walks, one limb serves as a mobile source of support while the other limb advances to a new support site. Then the limbs reverse their roles.

## 1. Introduction

This series of events is repeated by each limb until the person’s destination is reached. A single sequence of these functions of one limb is called a gait cycle (Perry 1992). Each gait cycle is usually divided into a stance and a swing phase. As described by Perry (Perry 1992), the stance phase is the period when the foot under consideration is in contact with the ground, approximately the first 60% of the gait cycle. The stance phase can be further subdivided into four phases: (1) loading response (0-10% of the gait cycle, beginning at initia contact) is the initial double stance period and continues until the contralatera foot is lifted for swing; (2) mid-stance (10-30% of the gait cycle) is the first part of the single-leg support period that begins as the contralateral foot is lifted and continues until body weight is aligned over the forefoot; (3) termina stance (30-50% of the gait cycle) is the second part of the single-leg support period that begins as heel rise and continues until the contralateral foot strikes the ground; and (4) pre-swing (50-60% of the gait cycle) is the second double support in the gait cycle that starts with initial contact of the contralateral foot and ends with ipsilateral toe-of. The swing phase is the rest of the gait cycle when the foot under consideration is not in contact with the ground. The swing phase can be subdivided into three phases: (1) initial swing (60-73% of the gait cycle) is approximately one-third of the swing period that begins with lift foot from ground and ends when the swinging foot is opposite the stance foot; (2) mid-swing (73-87% of the gait cycle) is the second part of the swing period that begins as the swinging foot is opposite the stance foot and ends with a vertica swinging tibia; and (3) terminal-swing (87-100% of the gait cycle) begins with a vertical tibia and ends when the foot strikes the ground.

During walking, muscles are sequentially activated in response to the stance and swing demands for weight acceptance, single limb support and limb advancement (Gage et al. 2009; Perry 1992). After initial contact, ankle dorsiflexors (tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, and extensor hallucis longus) work eccentrically to decelerate ankle plantarflexion and absorb the force of impact. Ankle dorsiflexors are also active during swing to prevent foot drag. Ankle plantarflexors (principally soleus) eccentrically contract during mid-stance to control forward motion of the tibia. During terminal stance, the action of the plantarflexors (soleus and gastrocnemius primarily) switch from eccentric to concentric to facilitate heel rise and reach peak plantarflexion moment. In late pre-swing the contraction of plantarflexors reduce rapidly with the rapid decline of ground reaction force (GRF), as the loading transfers to the other limb during pre-swing (Wang 2012).

Knee extensors (quadriceps) are active in terminal swing to pre-position the foot before contact, and in the loading response to absorb shock while maintaining knee stability. Knee flexors (hamstrings primarily) act mainly to protect potential knee hyper-extension in terminal swing and early stance, and to bend the knee in initial swing to prevent foot drag. Hip extensors (primarily adductor magnus and gluteus maximus) are active during terminal swing and initia contact to terminate and control hip flexion, and during loading response to advance the trunk over the leg. Hip flexors (primarily iliopsoas) contract to flex the hip during swing. Hip abductors (primarily gluteus maximus and gluteus medius) stabilize the hip laterally from rapid weight in early stance. Hip adductor muscles can also contribute to hip flexion in early swing (Perry 1992; Gutierrez 2003).

## 1.2. Pathological gait due to muscle weakness

Neurological disorders can impair a person’s ability to walk, and difer markedly in their primary pathology. The abnormalities they impose on walking mechanics are characterized into four functional categories: deformity, muscle weakness, impaired control and pain (Perry 1992). In this thesis, we are mainly concerned with the pathological gait due to muscle weakness. People with muscle weakness may not be able to generate the required torques for meeting the demands of daily activities, which can adversely impact their ability to be independent and their quality of life. More than an inconvenience, muscle weakness can have a domino efect on a person’s health; it is frequently followed by, for instance, slow metabolism, increase joint pressure, and postural instability (Horlings et al. 2008).

## 1.2.1. Hip abductors weakness

When there is hip abductor dysfunction, the body is less able to maintain the center of gravity on the side of the stance leg, and can lead to a drop of the pelvis towards the swing side, called a positive Trendelenburg sign. If the weakness is unilateral, the person may lean the trunk laterally toward the weak stance limb throughout the stance phase. Bilateral weakness may lead to lateral trunk lean towards the stance leg for both sides. In the long term, these compensation postures may contribute to a lateral spinal curve.

## 1.2.2. Hip extensor weakness

With hip extensor weakness, one may not be able to counteract external the hip flexor moment and may thus have a tendency for excessive hip flexion. To compensate for hip extensor weakness, one might lean the trunk posteriorly to position the GRF vector behind the hip joint center, which may over a long time lead to excessive lumbar lordosis.

## 1.2.3. Hip flexor weakness

With weakness of the hip flexors, the body may not have adequate hip flexion in early swing, potentially resulting in insuficient momentum for foot clearance and short step lengths. To compensate for hip flexor weakness, one might use the following strategies: posterior pelvis tilt, excessive knee flexion, and contralateral trunk lean.

## 1.2.4. Knee extensor weakness

If the knee extensors (quadriceps) are weak and have insuficient strength to support a flexed knee, it can be dificult or impossible to recover from any knee flexion during stance. Some strategies may be adopted to compensate for knee extensor weakness: (1) to contract hip extensors to retract the thigh and contract plantarflexors to prevent tibia advancement, resulting in knee extension or hyperextensio; (2) to lean the trunk forward to shift the GRF vector anterior to the knee, creating internal flexor moment, which may also lead to knee hyperextension; and (3) to turn the limb outward to lock the knee joint in extension. These compensations may result in a degeneration of the ligaments.

## 1.2.5. Knee flexor weakness

With knee flexor weakness, one may not be able to provide adequate knee flexion moment to restrain the thigh in terminal swing and early stance and to control the shank deceleration in the swing phase. Compensatory strategies may include increased hip flexion in the swing phase or a circumduction pattern of the swing leg.

## 1.2.6. Ankle plantarflexor weakness

With ankle plantarflexor weakness, one may not be able to counteract the external dorsiflexor moment during stance, which may result in uncontrolled tibial advancement, leading to a crouch gait position with flexed hips, flexed knees and excessive ankle dorsiflexion position during the weight bearing period. This may lead to increased activity of the quadriceps, prolonged heel contact during stance phase, short step lengths and/or slow walking speed.

## 1.2.7. Ankle dorsiflexor weakness

With ankle dorsiflexor weakness, one may be unable to counteract the externa plantarflexor moment during stance and may have dificulty achieving foot clearance during swing. This may result in forefoot or wholefoot contact at initial contact and a dropfoot pattern during swing. Compensations can include increased hip and knee flexion during swing to clear the foot.

## 1.3. Computational methods in joint torque prediction

## 1.3.1. Neuromusculoskeletal (NMS) modeling

NMS models are physics-based mathematical representations of a person’s skeletal anatomy and physiological mechanisms. Such models can help understand how muscles coordinate to generate movement and can be used to estimate joint torques during various tasks (Valero-Cuevas et al. 2009). Electromyography (EMG)-informed NMS models are commonly used to perform forward dynamics simulations of muscle dynamics and estimate joint torques using experimentally measured EMG as an input (Pizzolato et al. 2019). As described by Pizzolato et al. (Pizzolato et al. 2015), EMG-informed NMS models usually have three neural solution modes: EMG-driven, EMG-hybrid, and EMG-assisted, based on how experimental EMG signals are used to compute muscle excitation in the NMS model.

• EMG-driven: Only experimentally recorded EMG signals are used to compute muscle excitations that drive the model and to estimate musculotendon forces and joint torque.

• EMG-hybrid: Since EMG signals of some muscles are not practical or possible to routinely collect, the muscle excitation patterns of those muscles are constructed using an optimization algorithm, such as static optimization, by minimizing the sum of squared muscle activations with known motion (Anderson and Pandy 2001). Therefore, experimentally recorded EMG signals and muscle excitation patterns from optimization are combined to compute muscle excitation in this mode.

• EMG-assisted: As the quality of EMG signals may be afected by cross-talk and electrode placement, the EMG-assisted mode allows opti mization to adjust muscle excitations to better track joint torque.

Among the three modes, the EMG-driven mode is adequate for joints in which EMG signals of dominant muscle are easily recorded with good quality, such as knee and ankle joints (Sartori et al. 2014; Pizzolato et al. 2015). The EMGhybrid mode can compensate for the inability to access deep muscles such as the iliacus and psoas for hip joint motion, and the EMG-assisted mode can further compensate for the cross-talk or noisy EMGs (Hoang et al. 2018). In these situations, the EMG-hybrid and EMG-assisted models can more accurately represent the physiological properties of the NMS system; however, the two models involve an optimization algorithm to obtain the muscle activation of some muscles, which may be time-consuming and thus less suitable for realtime applications. On the other hand, the EMG-driven NMS model has been reported to robustly predict joint torque (Durandau et al. 2017), joint contact force (Pizzolato et al. 2017b) and joint stifness (Yao et al. 2018) in real-time.

With multiple artificial neurons interconnected to transmit information, an ANN is formed. Typically, an ANN is made of an input layer, hidden layer $/ \mathrm { s }$ and an output layer. The input layer takes in the inputs data. The hidden layer $/ \mathrm { s }$ receives the information from the input layer, processes it, and sent it to the output layer. The MLP, the most common type of ANN, can extract features and store information on the entire network to predict very complex functions, provided enough training data is available, without constructing complex mathematically explicit formulas and costly physical models. It is thus an eficient approach to model non-linear and complex problems and has shown great power in a number of applications in biomechanics. MLPs have been successfully applied to estimate ankle moments and ground reaction forces during walking and calf raises (Jacobs and Ferris 2015), elbow torque during elbow flexion (Jali et al. 2015), muscle force during dynamic contraction (Ba and Chew 2013), as well as energy expenditure during locomotion (Slade et al. 2019).

In contrast to MLPs, RNNs can model the temporal information inside a sequence, especially the relationship of the current input and former input. For this reason, it is often said that RNNs have memory. This use of “memory” makes RNNs advantageous for time series data, but introduces dificulties in mode training. One problem is that it cannot store past information for very long, due to gradients vanishing or exploding. A variation of an RNN was proposed to address this problem – a long short-term memory memory (LSTM) network (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997). ## 1.3.3. Transfer learning

Traditional ANN generally works well under a common assumption that the training and testing data have the same feature space and distribution. However, if the distribution of test data is diferent from training data, the prediction results are likely to degrade due to the diferences. It can furthermore be dificult or expensive to recollect training data and re-train models (Pan and Yang 2009; Weiss et al. 2016; Tan et al. 2018). In such cases, transfer learning strategies have been attracting much attention to address these problems. Transfer learning is inspired by the fact that humans can learn new tasks more quickly if they have previously learned similar knowledge (Pan and Yang 2009) whereas traditiona ANNs learn models independently for diferent tasks (Fig. 1.2). Transfer learning is generally categorized into three sub-settings: inductive, transductive and unsupervised transfer learning, based on diferent situations between the source and target domains and tasks. A domain D consists of: a feature space X and a marginal probability distribution P(X), where $X = \{ x _ { 1 } , . . . , x _ { n } \} \in { \mathcal { X } }$ Given a specific domain $\mathcal { D } = \{ \mathcal { X } , P ( X ) \}$ , a task $\mathcal { T } = \{ \mathcal { V } , f ( x ) \}$ consists of two parts: a label space Y and an objective predictive function $f : \mathcal { X }  \mathcal { Y } _ { : }$ , where the function f is used to predict the corresponding label f(x) of a new instance x (Lin and Jung 2017).

• Inductive transfer learning: the target task is diferent but related to the source task, regardless of the similarities between source and target domains.

• Transductive transfer learning: the target task is the same as the source task wherein the target domain is diferent but related to the source domain.

• Unsupervised transfer learning: the target task and domain are both diferent but related to the source task and domain.

Based on “what to transfer”, transfer learning approaches can be summarized into four cases in the above three sub-settings.

• Instance transfer: to re-weight some labeled instances in the source domain for learning in the target domain.

• Feature representation transfer: to find a “good” feature presentation of both source and target domains that reduces the diference between domains and the model predictive errors.

• Parameter transfer: to discover and transfer some shared parameters or prior knowledge between source and target domain models.

• Relational transfer: to build and transfer the relational knowledge mapping between the source and target domains.

Among these four, instance transfer and parameter transfer approaches are the two most popularly applied in recent research fields to solve several problems, for example, to solve limited data caused by expensive data acquisition due to ethical issues with patient data or limited research resources, and to reduce time and efort used in full re-calibration, rebuilding, or re-training for a new but related study. An instance transfer approach was incorporated into a generative adversarial network to provide enough data to train a classifier in human activity recognition systems on a target subject by transferring generated adaptive data based on source domain (subject) to the target domain (subject) (Soleiman and Nazerfard 2021). This study represented a cutting-edge solution for crosssubject knowledge transfer in human activity recognition when data acquisition and labeling are unavailable for new-users. A parameter transfer approach was adopted into LSTM neural network models to estimate skeletal muscle forces (Dao 2019), and improve prediction performance by transferring knowledge from a pre-trained LSTM model into a new model. A coupling approach of LSTM and transfer learning can potentially be applied to real-time skeletal muscle forces estimation in functional rehabilitation.

## 1.4. Wearable exoskeletons

In recent decades, there has been an increasing interest in developing wearable exoskeletons to assist people with movement disorders in rehabilitation and/or in daily activities. Wearable exoskeletons are very promising assistive/rehabilitative devices that can help enhance motor capacity in people with strength deficits or motor disorders. The most concerning and dificult aspects are to control the exoskeleton to assist the users and to achieve a comfortable interface between the exoskeleton and the user.

## 1.4.1. Control strategies

During exoskeleton-assisted rehabilitation training, assistive strategies can be categorized into: passive control, triggered passive control and assist-as-needed control (Proietti et al. 2016; Marchal-Crespo and Reinkensmeyer 2009; Meng et al. 2015).

• Passive control refers to “exoskeleton-in-charge”; exoskeletons are driven to guide the user to follow predefined trajectories or assistive forces/torques previously extracted from healthy populations. The user only passively participates in the training exercise. This passive control technique is commonly used at early stages of therapy when an impaired limb may be unresponsive.

• Triggered passive control is slightly diferent from the passive control, as the user triggers the exoskeleton assistance. After triggering, the exoskeleton is usually controlled passively along predetermined trajectories. This technique is frequently used to introduce the brain–machine interface into the control loop to assist people with nonrecoverable impairments, such as tetraplegia (Proietti et al. 2016).

• Assist-as-needed control refers to “user-in-charge”, “partially assistive control” and “active control”; users are dominant in performing daily tasks wherein exoskeletons provide assistance based on their intention and ability to generate torque, potentially stimulating neuroplasticity. This active control technique is usually adopted for patients with some motor capacity.

## 1.4. Wearable exoskeletons

With passive control, the person is not involved in making any efort to complete tasks, preventing the exoskeletons from facilitating the activity-driven neuroplastic changes required for recovery. On the other hand, passive assistance may be needed at an early stage of therapy when motor capacity is insuficient to participate/move actively. Passive assistance early in therapy can also be useful in reducing failures, earning users’ confidence and preventing negative reinforcement. As soon as the person has recovered a minimal amount of motor capacity, it is essential for neurorehabilation to involve the patient in the training process (Proietti et al. 2016).

This primary focus and context in thesis are active control techniques wherein an exoskeleton should compensate the user’s existing but insuficient muscle contributions. To provide assistive torque based on the user’s movement intention requires accurate and robust decoding of motor function, for instance, by recording underlying neuromuscular activities, such as brain, nerve and muscle EMG signals from the central nervous system (CNS). EMG signals are commonly used to predict the user’s movement intention detection and required joint torques, as they are relatively easy to acquire and process compared to brain and nerve signals, and as they provide essential information on human motion (Sartori et al. 2018).

## Specific aims

The scope of the thesis is about biomechanical parameter estimation for wearable exoskeleton system design. This thesis is based on five studies that focus on diferent aspects of exoskeleton controller design and physical design, within a context of contributing to the design of wearable exoskeleton systems for reha bilitation and movement assistance. Study I: Ankle Joint Torque Estimation using an EMG-Driven Neuromusculoskeletal Model and an Artificial Neural Network Model

Motivation: Both EMG-driven NMS and ANN model-based methods have been applied frequently in limb joint torque estimation, but their respective advantages in prediction have not been well investigated.

Objective: To estimate ankle joint torque using an EMG-driven NMS mode and a standard-ANN model simultaneously.

Study II: Ankle Joint Torque Prediction using an NMS Solver-Informed ANN Model

Motivation: Results from Study I suggested that the standard ANN mode could predict ankle torques more accurately than NMS models when trained on a large and varied set of trials, whereas the NMS model in ankle torque prediction was more accurate for movements that have similar muscle contraction coordination as in the calibration movements and possibly more useful due to its descriptions of underlying physical principles. Objective: To estimate ankle joint torque by an NMS solver-informed ANN model (hybrid-ANN) and investigate the generalizability of the hybrid-ANN model across subjects.

## 3.3. Data processing (Studies I, II and III)

EMG signals were processed by band-pass filtering (30-300 Hz), rectifying, low-pass filtering (6 Hz), and normalizing to the maximum EMG value obtained from processed EMGs across all trials (Pizzolato et al. 2017a; Sartori et al. 2016; Hoang et al. 2018).

Joint angles were computed using an inverse kinematics method in which a weighted least square problem that minimizes the distance between modeled and measured markers is solved. Joint torques were computed through the inverse dynamics method by solving the dynamic equation of motion (Eq. (3.1)) (Zajac et al. 2002)

$$
\mathbf {M} (q) \ddot {q} + \mathbf {C} (q, \dot {q}) + \mathbf {G} (q) = \tau ,\tag{3.1}
$$

where $q , \dot { q } , \ddot { q }$ are the position, velocity, and acceleration of the generalized coordinates, respectively. $\mathbf { M } ( q )$ is the mass matrix, ${ \bf C } ( { q } , \dot { { q } } )$ is the centripetal and Coriolis forces matrix, $\mathbf { G } ( q )$ is the gravitational forces matrix, and τ is the vector of unknown generalized forces.

## 3.4. Joint torque prediction models

## 3.4.1. EMG-driven neuromusculoskeletal model (Studies I and II)

We used an open-source EMG-informed NMS model developed by Pizzolato et al. (Pizzolato et al. 2015) consisting of four elements (see Fig. 2 in Paper

I): muscle activation dynamics, muscle contraction dynamics, musculotendon kinematics, and joint dynamics (Sartori et al. 2011).

The muscle activation dynamics element is used to compute muscle activation with acquired EMG signals as input. The acquired EMG signal $e ( t )$ is related to neural activation $u ( t )$ in a time domain $t ,$ as Eq. (3.2) (Lloyd and Besier 2003).

$$
u (t) = \alpha e (t - d) - \beta_ {1} u (t - 1) - \beta_ {2} u (t - 2),\tag{3.2}
$$

where $\alpha$ is the muscle gain coeficient; d is the electromechanical delay; and $\beta _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 }$ are the recursive coeficients. The neural activation is related to muscle activation $a ( t )$ and the relationship is formulated as Eq. (3.3):

$$
a (t) = \frac {e ^ {A u (t)} - 1}{e ^ {A} - 1},\tag{3.3}
$$

where A is the non-linear shape factor (Buchanan et al. 2004; Hoang et al. 2018).

The muscle contraction dynamics element is used to calculate musculotendon forces using a Hill-type muscle model. The relationship between force in each musculotendon unit (MTU) $F ^ { m t }$ , muscle activation and kinematics is defined as Eq. (3.4),

$$
F ^ {m t} = F _ {0} ^ {m} [ f _ {a} (\tilde {l} _ {m}) \cdot f _ {v} (\tilde {v} _ {m}) \cdot a + f _ {p} (\tilde {l} _ {m}) + d _ {m} \tilde {v} _ {m} ] c o s (\phi),\tag{3.4}
$$

where $F _ { 0 } ^ { m }$ is the maximum isometric muscle force, $\tilde { l } _ { m }$ is the fiber length, $f _ { a } ( \tilde { l } _ { m } )$ describes the active force-length relationship, $\tilde { v } _ { m }$ is fiber contraction velocity, $f _ { v } ( \tilde { v } _ { m } )$ describes the force-velocity relationship, $f _ { p } ( \tilde { l } _ { m } )$ is the passive force-length relationship, $\phi$ is the fiber pennation angle, and $d _ { m }$ is the muscle damping parameter.

The musculotendon kinematics element is used to calculate moment arms and musculotendon lengths of each MTU using a musculoskeletal geometric model. Finally, the joint dynamics element is used to predict joint torques through the product of musculotendon forces $F _ { m t }$ and their moment arms $r _ { m t }$

## 3.4.2. Artificial neural network models (Studies I, II and III)

## 3.4.2.1. Feed-forward ANN/MLP models (Studies I and II)

We constructed a standard feed-forward ANN/MLP model to predict ankle joint torque in Study I with the same inputs that we used in the EMG-driven NMS model, specifically EMG signals and joint angle (see Fig. 3 in Study I). Since the standard feed-forward ANN technique is a purely data-driven method and would become less reliable when encountering unseen movements, we constructed a hybrid-ANN model in Study II that leverages more informative physical features, specifically ankle joint torque and muscle forces computed by the NMS solver (see Fig. 3 in Study II).

## 3. Methods

The feed-forward ANN models consist of an input neural layer, n hidden layers and an output neural layer. The standard-ANN model included four neurons in the input layer, which are experimentally measured EMG data from three muscles and ankle joint angle. Besides these input variables, hybrid-ANN added four additional input features: ankle joint torque and three muscle forces computed by the NMS solver. For the intra-subject scenario, two hidden layers were used; four hidden layers were used in the inter-subject scenario. In the output layer, the predicted ankle joint torque was determined. The mean square error between predicted and experimentally measured ankle joint torque is used during the training process. The hyper-parameters of the ANN models were determined by “coarse-to-fine” random search (Bergstra and Bengio 2012).

## 3.4.2.2. Long short-term memory models (Study III)

We constructed LSTM neural networks to predict joint torque based on EMG signals and joint angles (see Fig. 2 in Study III). The EMG signals and joint angles were acquired from 3D motion analysis and pre-processed as input data for the LSTM neural networks. For each LSTM neural network, the time-series input data were transformed as time slices data. Each time slice included five time steps, and each time step had seventeen features including thirteen muscle EMG signals and four joint angles. n LSTM layers (n = 2 for single-task and n = 3 for multiple-task in the intra-subject scenario; n = 3 for single-task and n = 4 for multiple-task in the inter-subject scenario were included, followed by a dense layer and a dropout layer. The joint torque was predicted at the output layer. The hyper-parameters of the LSTM models were also determined by “coarse-to-fine” random search.

## 3.5. Transfer learning (Studies II and III)

Generally, trained models in inter-task/subject scenarios would face a per formance drop when tested with unseen data from new tasks and/or users (Soleimani and Nazerfard 2021; Su and M Gutierrez-Farewik 2020). To boost the prediction performance, transfer learning methods (see Fig. 4 in Study II and Fig. 3 in Study III) are used in the inter-task/inter-subject scenarios to learn structural similarities by pre-training a model on multiple subjects/tasks and transfer the learned knowledge to a target task/subject. In the pre-trained model, we extracted the layers except for last several layers and transferred them to the target model. In the target model, we added new layers, and then trained the weights of new layers and fine-tuned the weights of other layers with the data from the target task/subject. The fine-tuning technique is an optiona step in transfer learning and can potentially achieve meaningful improvements by incrementally adapting the pre-trained features to the new data (Shin et al. 2016).

## 3.6. Human-machine system (Studies IV and V)

We created a human knee exoskeleton CAD model, then incorporated it into a musculoskeletal model (AnyBody AMMR Twente Lower Extremity Model Version 2.0) (Carbone et al. 2015), creating a virtual HMS.

The CAD (Solidworks) model was made based on a physical knee exoskeleton, consisting of a Brushless DC motor (EC 90 flat 90 Watt, Maxon, Switzerland), a gear reducer (Planetary Gearhead GP 52C, Maxon, Switzerland), a bevel gear to change the direction of applied torque, thigh and shank cufs, knee joint connection, and strap bases. The varied assistive torque generated by the exoskeleton was modeled as an ideal torque. Mass, moment of inertia and damping of the exoskeleton components were incorporated into the virtual HMS.

In the virtual HMS, three diferent assistive strategies, namely a rotationa actuator (RA), a simple pendulum model (SPM) and a damped pendulum mode (DPM), were used to simulate assistive torques at the knee during normal and fast gait. Outcome measures including muscle impulse, required physiologica knee torque, joint contact forces and interaction forces were estimated through inverse dynamic analysis, with muscle recruitment and activation resolved through static optimization with an assumption of minimizing the sum of the squared muscle activation (Damsgaard et al. 2006; Bassani et al. 2017; Rasmussen et al. 2001).

Four knee exoskeleton CAD models were also developed in the virtual HMS to study the efects of weight distributions of the knee exoskeleton components on simulated muscle activities during three functional movements, i.e., normal gait, squat, and ramp ascent (8°).

## 4.1.2. A combined NMS and standard-ANN model (Study II)

The main contribution of this study is that we proposed an NMS solver-informed ANN model to predict ankle joint torques, leveraging the advantages of both models. This study investigated the joint torque performance of three models in two scenarios: intra-subject and inter-subject tests (Table 4.2). In the intersubject tests, we adopted a transfer learning technique to further improve the prediction accuracy by taking advantage of the information extracted from previous subjects. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to apply an NMS solver-informed ANN model to predict joint torques, and the first attempt to adopt a transfer learning approach into ANN models to predict torques in diferent/unseen subjects during daily activities.

Overall, the hybrid-ANN model predicted ankle joint torques more accurately than both the NMS and the standard-ANN model, presumably attributable to the additional physical features obtained by the NMS model. The important physical features, i.e., individual muscle forces and joint torque, extracted from the NMS model into the ANN model design, presumably improved the model’s performance in predicting joint torque; individual muscle forces are the dominant intermediate components in joint torque computation based on the joint dynamics model (Fig. 2 in Study II) and the joint torques from the NMS-solver were able to provide more relevant physiological features to train the hybrid-ANN model. These features can complement standard ANN models that might become less accurate when encountering unseen movements. For example, compared to hybrid-ANN and NMS models, the lowest torque prediction accuracy was observed in the standard-ANN for unseen tested movements— for instance, slow walking in Case 2 in the intra-subject test (Fig. 6 $H _ { 2 }$ in Study II).

The torques predicted by the NMS model displayed small ofsets at the beginning of the cycle, for example, $G _ { 2 }$ in Fig 6, and $A _ { 2 }$ and $C _ { 2 }$ in Fig $^ { 7 }$ of Study II, show some ofsets at the beginning of the cycle when the measured torque was close to zero. This may be attributable to that fact that the two previous timesteps of muscle-tendon unit neural activation were needed to compute muscle neural activation (Eq. (3.2)), but at the beginning of the cycle, any earlier neural activation values were not available. Instead, they were approximated using EMG signals from two previous time-steps and may thus have resulted in the observed ofset in predicted torque. This ofset was eliminated in the hybrid-ANN, which can be regarded as the correction mapping from joint torque predicted by the NMS model to the measured torque. That is possible because, compared to NMS, the hybrid-ANN model has one more step: adding neura networks to better train a model by minimizing the errors between measured and predicted joint torques.

Our findings suggest that the NMS solver-informed ANN model predicted ankle joint torque more accurately than both the NMS and the standard ANN models, and indicate a potential benefit of integrating features of the NMS model into standard ANN models.

## 4.1.3. LSTM model (Study III)

The main contribution and novelty of this study was analysis of LSTM mode robustness in predicting joint torques several joints and planes during daily activities using LSTM neural networks and transfer learning (Table 4.3) and the evaluation of the generalizability of LSTM networks to predict torques across subjects and across movements/“tasks”. In this study, ankle and knee joint torques in the sagittal plane, as well as hip joint torques in both sagitta and frontal planes during several diferent motions were predicted using LSTM neural network models in intra- and inter-subject scenarios, and in intra- and inter-task tests. We constructed diferent LSTM networks in diferent cases to predict joint torques, and investigated their generalizability across movements and subjects. Our findings, particularly those relating to how to create and train generalizable models with inputs from few movements, can provide usefu guidelines for training models when only minimal amounts of movement input for training are feasible. This model thus holds great promise in applications of assistance-as-needed exoskeleton control design, in which the intended users may not be able to undergo an extensive battery of movements to train the model.

The LSTM models predicted multiple joint torques accurately both through a uniform model trained by multiple-tasks and through ten separate models trained by a single task each in intra-subject tests (Case 1 and Case 2 in Figure 5 in Study III). Both of these models were tested with the unseen, “new” trials of the same movement types used for training. Generally, artificial neural networks can be expected to predict accurately when trained and tested on the same motions. The constructed LSTM model with multiple layers and neurons, as one type of artificial neural networks, is capable of selectively remembering patterns for a duration of time, and could extract the complex nonlinear relationship between EMG signals and joint torques, and thus accurately predict torques in unseen trials of the same movement types, regardless of whether the network consists of a uniform model or of ten separate models.

Our results illustrate that both a uniform model trained on all ten movements and ten separate LSTM models trained on one movement each could predict lower limb joint torques accurately, i.e. with low prediction error.

## 4.1.4. Transfer learning (Studies II and III)

Transfer learning techniques were adopted to improve the joint torque prediction accuracy across subjects in Studies II and III and across tasks in Study III (Table 4.2 and 4.3). Transfer learning is a popular technique in cross-subject or cross-task validation, and can prevent the performance drop mentioned earlier by adapting obtained knowledge from the source domain (previous subjects/tasks) to the target domain (new subject/task) (Soleimani and Nazerfard 2021). ## 4.2. Human-machine system (Studies IV and V)

## 4.2.1. Evaluation of control strategies influence on the user (Study IV)

The main contribution of this study was the development of a virtual HMS that fully incorporates both a knee exoskeleton hardware and control into a detailed musculoskeletal model, and its demonstrated use in computing how diferent assistive strategies may afect the user’s required torques, muscle impulses, contact forces and interactive forces during movement. Detailed information at joint and muscle levels provided by using a musculoskeleta modeling environment can be important factors in optimal controller design of exoskeletons.

To illustrate the potential use of the developed virtual HMS, we focused on the knee in normal gait, and adopted three common but conceptually diferent assistive strategies at the knee joint (RA, SPM and DPM strategies) to determine how they may influence the user. We found that compared to no assistance, assistance torque based on DPM models can reduce the demand on the user’s knee flexors while the RA-based model reduced the demand on knee extensors by a small amount (Fig. 5 in Study IV). This finding illustrates that the knee exoskeleton with the DPM-based assistive strategy could, for instance, assist a person with weakness in the knee flexors to achieve normal and even fast walking kinematics, despite the weakness.

Despite the lower demand on the user’s muscles in SPM and DPM assistance modes than in other modes, it is notable that the interactive forces between the user and the exoskeleton straps were higher in the pendulum-based models in late stance and late swing (Fig. 7 in Study IV). In addition, we found the highest compressive tibiofemoral force and sartorius and hamstrings impulses during late stance and late swing in the no-assistance mode (Fig. 6 in Study IV). The comparatively lower compressive tibiofemoral force in the SPM and DPM modes can be in part attributed to the reduced muscle impulse during late stance and late swing. High compressive tibiofemoral force has frequently been used as an indicator in gait analysis (Mannisi et al. 2019; Lee and Wang 2015; Wang et al. 2016) associated with knee pain.

The results of the illustrative example suggest that both joint position and rotational direction should be considered in an assistive strategy, and that a damping component in a pendulum-based assistive strategy could be beneficial to further reduce the user’s required muscle efort during the stance phase. We have also demonstrated that a higher assistive torque may incur a trade-of in the form of higher interaction forces, which should be kept in mind when designing a control strategy.

## 4.2.2. Diferent weight distributions of a knee exoskeleton: efect on users’ muscle activation (Study V)

Studies of powered exoskeletons often focus on technological aspects such as actuators, control methods, energy and efects on gait. However, limited research has been conducted on how diferent mechanical design parameters can afect the user. The main contribution of this study was the analyses of how diferent mechanical designs of a knee exoskeleton influence the user’s required efort during diferent movements, using a combination of simulation and experimentation. In a simulation with a virtual HMS, four realistic knee exoskeleton CAD models were developed and their efects on simulated muscle activities were studied during three functional movements. The detailed information of how a powered exoskeleton’s core components may afect a user’s ability and performance can contribute to exoskeletons’ physical design for diferent daily activities.

We have shown through simulations that exoskeleton physical designs and weight distributions influence muscle activities in diferent ways during diferent movements. No single physical design was optimal for all movements. Efects of an exoskeleton’s distribution of bulky components are not negligible. Furthermore, an exoskeleton did not always induce more muscle activities than without, which indicates that muscle synergy patterns could be altered with the exoskeleton, and that its weight can be advantageous in some instances/movements. In addi tion, the robustness of our simulation was tested by comparing their agreement with experimental results of subjects donning one of the physical designs while performing two of the simulated motions.

## 4. Results and Discussion

Our findings reveal that exoskeleton weight distribution can influence muscle activities, and that the influence is movement-dependent. No single physica design was optimal for all movements. A user’s daily routine, activity goals and residual muscle strength should be considered while designing an exoskeleton’s physical design and control algorithm.

## 4.3. Limitations

There are of course some limitations in this thesis. We only constructed one NMS model to predict joint torque. In addition, a subset sensitivity analysis of some parameters in the NMS model, such as electromechanical delay, muscle damping coeficient and tendon types, can be further investigated to determine the optimal parameters for joint torque prediction. Only MLP and LSTM neura networks were investigated; more complicated architectures, such as convolutions neural networks, can be further investigated for joint torque prediction. The performance of using diferent neural networks can be further compared and analyzed. In the virtual HMS, only three control strategies of exoskeleton’s efect on the user was analyzed during gait; it should be technically possible to analyze a greater number of control strategies in the HMS. The strap’s tension, material, and surface friction were not considered in the HMS, nor were the soft tissue properties of the user’s thigh and shank, which will al afect the computed interaction forces. In the experimental movements, one could consider standardizing the chair height in relation to each subject’s height during sit-to-stand and stand-to-sit, and could also take into account diferent subjects’ squat techniques, as they can result in diferent muscle activation patterns. Also, this study only includes studies of able-bodied persons, even though the context in focus, exoskeleton users, will largely consist of patient populations. Furthermore, the able-bodied subjects enrolled in this thesis were relatively homogeneous with regards to age and overall fitness.

# Conclusions and future work

This thesis presented studies in which we robustly and accurately estimate joint torque using machine learning models and neuromusculoskeletal models in daily activities. The detailed analysis of diferent models’ performance in predicting joint torques can contribute to determining which model to choose, and under which circumstances, and thus be of large benefit for exoskeleton rehabilitation controller design. This thesis also analyzed diferent assistive strategies of an exoskeleton’s influence on users as well as the efects of diferent weight distributions of a exoskeleton on users, which makes it possible to estimate biomechanical parameters early in the design process. This can ultimately minimize prototype iterations and evaluate parameters that are otherwise dificult to measure experimentally.

The standard ANN/MLP model predicted ankle torques somewhat better when trained on a large and varied set of trials but less accurately in unseen movements whereas the NMS model was overall more useful due to the underlying physical principles. The combination model of NMS and standard-ANN, i.e., NMS solver informed-ANN model, had a better torque prediction performance than both the NMS and the standard ANN models, indicating the potentia benefit of integrating physical features from NMS model into standard ANN models. Furthermore, constructed LSTM models illustrated both a uniform model trained on several movements and several separate LSTM models trained on one movement each could predict lower limb joint torques accurately. By incorporating transfer learning in inter-subject and inter-task scenarios, and thereby making use of knowledge learned from previous users/tasks, generalizability of both MLP and LSTM models improved. The implications of this is that since joint torque was accurately predicted with little training data from new subjects, this method should be feasible to predict torque even when new subjects, for example people with motor disorders, are unable perform a wide range of movements in the training process.

The developed virtual HMS makes it possible to compute torques, muscle impulse, contact forces and interactive forces involved in simulated movements with diferent knee exoskeleton assistive strategies and diferent weight distribu tions.
