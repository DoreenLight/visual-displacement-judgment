## Abstract
Hand impairments caused by stroke or neuromuscular disorders severely restrict daily activities, underscoring the need for effective, home-applicable rehabilitation systems. This study presents HandMaid, a wearable hand exoskeleton that restores motion through electromyography (EMG)-based intention detection and an active backdrive safety mechanism. The system integrates a 3D-printed rigid exoskeleton, linear actuators, and a neural network interface that is retrained before each use to compensate for variations in electrode placement, skin resistance, temperature, and humidity. This adaptive training procedure ensures reliable signal interpretation under different environmental and physiological conditions. Kinematic and experimental analyses demonstrate that the device covers approximately 81% of the anatomical range of motion (MCP \\(\approx \\) 50\\(^{\circ }\\), PIP \\(\approx \\) 95\\(^{\circ }\\), DIP \\(\approx \\) 90\\(^{\circ }\\)), while maintaining a mean classification accuracy of 98.1 ± 1.6% and an average response latency of \\(\approx \\)1.05 s. The threshold-based active backdrive logic provides immediate reversal in case of user resistance or discomfort, ensuring both safety and comfort during use. Weighing around 400 g and with a total production cost below $350, HandMaid offers a portable, low-cost, and anatomically consistent rehabilitation solution. The results confirm that the system enables user-safe, adaptive, and cost-effective robotic rehabilitation suitable for both clinical and home environments, while its retrainable EMG model ensures robustness against session-to-session variability.

##  1 Introduction
Our hands are among the most crucial tools that connect us to the physical world. They serve significant functions in communication, expressing emotions, and understanding the individuals around us. Injuries resulting in nerve, soft tissue, cartilage, tendon, or ligament damage; fractures; calcification; paralysis; postoperative complications; tunnel syndrome; and certain neurological conditions can lead to loss of function in the hands. The possibility of functional loss in the hands due to cerebrovascular events is also high, and increasing average human life span data suggest a higher likelihood of encountering such cases. To ensure efficient utilization of resources, the brain, as the organ that consumes the most energy in proportion to body mass, must use its resources as efficiently as possible. Adjacent regions take over cortical areas associated with an unused function, leading to irreversible structural changes in this process. Therefore, the prompt initiation of rehabilitation activities is crucial. According to WHO data, stroke is the second leading cause of death globally, with more than half of cases resulting in upper extremity impairments. Unfortunately, the recovery rate for patients experiencing hand function loss is below 50% [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR1 "Eschmann H, Héroux ME, Cheetham JH, Potts S, Diong J \(2019\) Thumb and finger movement is reduced after stroke: an observational study. PLoS ONE 14\(6\):1–14")]. In cases where quality of life is severely impacted, rehabilitation provides a crucial opportunity for patients to restore lost strength or reverse neurological deficits. Stroke patients often experience hyperexcitability, leading to tightness and muscle stiffness.
Given the challenges in achieving full recovery, early and intensive rehabilitation plays a vital role in maximizing functional outcomes and improving patients’ independence. Rehabilitation is also highly effective in alleviating this extremely distressing condition [[2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR2 "Wolf SL, Winstein CJ, Miller JP, Taub E, Uswatte G, Morris D \(2006\) Effect of constraint-induced movement therapy on upper extremity function 3 to 9 months after stroke: the excite randomized clinical trial. JAMA 296\(17\):2095–2104")]. The goal of rehabilitation is to strengthen muscles, engage joints, and promote neuroplasticity through repeated movements. Maintaining patient comfort throughout the process is essential to ensure consistency and long-term adherence. A rehabilitation program that follows a natural and structured progression has a higher chance of success. A study suggested that performing 12 repetitions per minute of flexion–extension exercises alone could contribute to recovery [[3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR3 "Wang D, Meng Q, Meng Q, Li X, Yu H \(2018\) Design and development of a portable exoskeleton for hand rehabilitation. IEEE Trans Neural Syst Rehabil Eng 26\(12\):2376–2386")]. If adequate stimuli are provided, the brain’s neuroplastic structure enables it to reorganize itself. Rehabilitation is an activity that involves processes such as repetitive physical activity and clinical assessment and requires intensive participation from the therapist. Access to physical therapy centers is not easy for all individuals in need, and the capacity of states with limited resources to meet all demands is often below the minimum level. Regularly performing exercises over a specific period can help regain neurological functions in the central nervous system and restore lost muscle strength. Robotic rehabilitation surpasses traditional methods by offering a more precise, consistent, and data-driven approach to therapy. Robots can perform repetitive movements with exceptional accuracy and without fatigue, enhancing the effectiveness of treatment. Their ability to collect objective data and provide real-time feedback allows for more accurate monitoring of patient progress. The continuous and intensive exercises facilitated by robotic systems promote faster recovery, while features like gamification and virtual reality help boost patient motivation and engagement. In neurological conditions such as stroke, early robotic-assisted therapy can accelerate neuroplasticity, leading to improved outcomes. Additionally, these systems alleviate the workload of physiotherapists, allowing them to treat more patients simultaneously while ensuring exercises are conducted in a safe and controlled manner. By offering personalized treatment programs tailored to individual needs, robotic rehabilitation provides a more structured, efficient, and sustainable recovery process, solidifying its role as a vital component of modern healthcare. Moreover, clinical studies have demonstrated certain advantages of robotic rehabilitation over traditional methods [[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR4 "Lo A, Guarino P, Richards L, Haselkorn J, Wittenberg G \(2010\) Robot-assisted therapy for long-term upper-limb impairment after stroke. N Engl J Med 362:1772–83. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa0911341
                  
                ")].
Rehabilitation devices facilitating finger bending via fluid systems may appear anatomically compatible but require a pressure source and lack portability. Cable- or tendon-driven devices are susceptible to sagging or entanglement, and in many cable-driven devices, actuators must be positioned outside the robot, complicating installation. The use of actuators outside the robot is common in many cable-driven devices [[5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR5 "Jian EK, Chan GD, Kok KT \(2018\) Wearable hand exoskeleton for activities of daily living. Paper presented at EEE-EMBS conference on biomedical engineering and sciences \(IECBES\), 3–6 Dec 2018"), [6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR6 "Jeong S, Trab P, Desai JP \(2020\) Integration of self-sealing suction cups on the flexotendon glove-ii robotic exoskeleton system. IEEE Robot Autom Lett 5\(2\):867–874")]. This external positioning not only complicates setup but also adds complexity to the system. Designs that occupy the palmar side of the hand, which is crucial for gripping or grasping, are often favored in tendon-driven robots [[7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR7 "Chen W, Li G, Li N, Yu P, Wang R \(2022\) Soft exoskeleton with fully actuated thumb movements for grasping assistance. IEEE Trans Robot 38\(4\):2194–2207")].
The design of hand rehabilitation robots poses several challenges, including the complex anatomy of the hand, its high mobility, and the limited space available for the device. The main focus for achieving a portable and ergonomic design should be on positioning it on either the top or bottom of the hand. Figure [1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#Fig1) presents a concise overview of design patterns commonly employed in academic publications and by businesses. Design objectives classify rehabilitation robots for the hand as rehabilitative, assistive, or haptic. Those designed for rehabilitation can guide users through predefined exercises, focus on completing specific tasks, or perform activities based on the user’s intention using biological signals such as EMG or EEG.

Task-oriented devices communicate with the external world through sensors. Sensors can detect various physical quantities such as force, displacement, position, angle, and pressure. For example, an end switch may be sufficient for a robot that repeats predefined tasks. Force, deformation, or optical sensors can measure interaction with an object. Furthermore, utilizing biological signals such as EMG and EEG can enhance flexibility. However, the limited implementation of force sensors often hinders interaction with objects, lacks intuitiveness, and requires wiring. Incorporating virtual reality (VR) applications and enriching content with gamelike features can make the rehabilitation process interactive and enjoyable [[8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR8 "Yang L, Zhang F, Zhu J, Fu Y \(2022\) A portable device for hand rehabilitation with force cognition: design, interaction, and experiment. IEEE Trans Cogn Dev Syst 14\(2\):599–607")]. Strain sensors can measure the applied force, allowing assessments of how much strength the hand has regained during the rehabilitation process.
There are three broad structural categories of models used and continuously developed for hand rehabilitation: Actuators transmit power to the fingers through rigid connecting elements in rigid/frame structures; through cables, pulleys, and sheaths in cable-driven models; and through fluid-filled soft exoskeletal devices to facilitate finger bending or stretching. Rigid exoskeletons, utilizing inelastic materials, allow easy placement of sensors and enable more stable control since the system’s response remains constant. These exoskeletons offer high customizability, can be produced from various materials including 3D-printable ones, require minimal maintenance, and have low failure probabilities. Their ability to operate with electric actuators, along with their rigid and simple structure, can make them fully portable, as they do not have high power requirements. The separation of the actuator and the hand device in cable-driven models benefits applications that require a high degree of freedom (DOF). However, predicting system response under load can be challenging due to the use of cables and pulleys, and issues such as backlash or retraction may lead to inconsistent outputs. They are also susceptible to tangling and require regular maintenance. Another similar approach, referred to as tendon-driven, constructs devices based on cables that mimic tendon-like structures. Tendon-based systems commonly use routing sheaths instead of pulley-like structures. Soft exoskeletal structures aim to mimic the movement of living organisms. While some designs use electric motors, pressurized air or liquid is more common. Although they provide an advantage by closely resembling the trajectory of human hand movement, they have limitations in applying force only to a specific point after a certain angle. Most of them require a pressurized source, reducing portability, increasing costs, and making the system less agile. Some designers aim to contribute to the rehabilitation process by using passive actuators such as springs or shape memory alloys [[9](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR9 "Chen T, Lum PS \(2016\) Hand rehabilitation after stroke using a wearable, high DOF, spring powered exoskeleton. Paper presented at the 3rd international 38th annual international conference of the IEEE engineering in medicine and biology society \(EMBC\), Orlando, FL, USA, 16–20 Aug 2016")].
Rehabilitation patients may experience discomfort and pain due to effects such as stiffness or locking when moving their joints over time. When such a situation occurs, the patient may either manually intervene in the robot or request the robot to detect the condition and stop its movement. In hand robots, the desired actuator features are small size, high power, and low speed, making the use of reducers inevitable and sacrificing backdrivability. Underactuated designs address this limitation to improve user comfort and safety. Underactuated robots allow for some external intervention rather than following a rigid trajectory solely guided by actuators. Unlike fully actuated robots, underactuated robots are not solely guided by actuators in determining their kinematics; they can also be moved passively to some extent. This provides an opportunity for intervention when the patient experiences discomfort or pain. Underactuated hand robots can be adapted to various types, using extra joints in rigid or frame structures [[10](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR10 "Sarac M, Solazzi M, Sotgiu E, Bergamasco M, Frisoli A \(2016\) Design and kinematic optimization of a novel underactuated robotic hand exoskeleton. Meccanica. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s11012-016-0530-z
                  
                ")], or employing cable-driven structures with series pulleys [[11](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR11 "Cempini M, De R, SMM, Lenz T, Cortese M, Giovacchini F \(2013\) Kinematics and design of a portable and wearable exoskeleton for hand rehabilitation. Paper presented at IEEE international conference on rehabilitation robotics 01 June 2013")]. Hybrid solutions with damping elements added between actuators and transmission mechanisms to smooth transitions are also available. The following sections explain the “active backdrive” method, a solution developed to address the backdrivability issue in undesired situations. The active backdriving feature, specifically implemented in this system, has not been integrated into conventional rehabilitation robots.

The devices differ significantly in their degrees of freedom (DOFs), actuation methods, achievable torque or force output, and backdrivability characteristics.
The RobHand system focuses on a five-DOF design but lacks backdrivability, relying on conventional DC motors. Bagneschi et al. introduced a tendon-driven structure with inherent compliance, achieving 10–15 N of grasp force while preserving natural movement through soft tendon routing. Overall, as summarized in the table, most existing systems present trade-offs between force output, compliance, and backdrivability. This highlights the need for designs—such as the proposed **HandMaid** system—that integrate high intention recognition accuracy with active backdrivability and ergonomic motion fidelity.
When adding human finger joints to the robot’s kinematics, designers can control them either independently or dependently. Independent control of joints might seem to offer more freedom, but anatomical dependence exists between them. Independent designs are suitable for larger, heavier, and non-portable robots, as they offer higher DOF but require more actuators [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR17 "Kawasaki H, Ito S, Ishigure Y, Nishimoto Y, Aoki T, Mouri T, Sakaeda H \(2007\) Development of a hand motion assist robot for rehabilitation therapy by patient self-motion control. Paper presented at the international conference on rehabilitation robotics, Noordwijk, Netherlands, 13–15 June 2007")]. The hand robot should operate in harmony with the anatomical joints to avoid causing any discomfort. While performing the flexion–extension movement, the robot’s joint and the anatomical joint can be designed to coincide or remain separate. Although aligning the joints may create a more esthetic appearance, it is quite challenging for all five fingers since they also utilize lateral areas [[18](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR18 "Chiri A, Giovacchini F, Vitiello N, Cattin E, Roccella S \(2009\) HANDEXOS: towards an exoskeleton device for the rehabilitation of the hand Streptomyces fradiae. Paper presented at EEE/RSJ international conference on intelligent robots and systems, St. Louis, MO, USA, 10–15 Oct 2009")]. Some designs offering a high degree of freedom compromise portability [[8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR8 "Yang L, Zhang F, Zhu J, Fu Y \(2022\) A portable device for hand rehabilitation with force cognition: design, interaction, and experiment. IEEE Trans Cogn Dev Syst 14\(2\):599–607")]. Certain models are limited to a single finger or a few fingers [[11](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR11 "Cempini M, De R, SMM, Lenz T, Cortese M, Giovacchini F \(2013\) Kinematics and design of a portable and wearable exoskeleton for hand rehabilitation. Paper presented at IEEE international conference on rehabilitation robotics 01 June 2013")], making them unsuitable for full-hand applications. Others, despite being designed for the entire hand, do not allow independent finger movement [[19](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR19 "Loconsole C, Leonardis D, Barsotti M, Solazzi M, Frisoli A \(2013\) An EMG-based robotic hand exoskeleton for bilateral training of grasp. Paper presented at world haptics conference \(WHC\) 14–17 April 2013")].
While it may seem logical to simplify the mechanism by involving only the MCP (metacarpophalangeal) joint, as seen in the RobHand design [[12](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR12 "Cisnal A, Pérez TJ, Fraile JC, Sierra D, Fuente E \(2021\) Robhand: a hand exoskeleton with real-time EMG-driven embedded control. Quantifying hand gesture recognition delays for bilateral rehabilitation. IEEE Access 9:137809–137823. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s41315-023-00282-2
                  
                ")], or by including both the MCP and PIP joints but excluding the DIP (distal interphalangeal) joint, as some designs do, such an approach disrupts the natural motion order. Similarly, designs that bend the distal phalanx and indirectly move the other joints [[20](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR20 "Sarakoglou I, Brygo A, Mazzanti D, Hernandez NG, Caldwell DG \(2016\) HEXOTRAC: a highly under-actuated hand exoskeleton for finger tracking and force feedback. Paper presented at IEEE/RSJ international conference on intelligent robots and systems \(IROS\), 9–14 Oct 2016")], as employed in many studies, are not adopted in this work. When joint movement follows the natural order, it is believed that this not only strengthens the muscles but also promotes neuroplasticity.
Despite technological advances, there remains no unified framework that combines mechanical design, EMG-based intention recognition, and patient-specific adaptive control within a low-cost, wearable, and backdrivable hand exoskeleton. The problem addressed in this study, therefore, is to design and validate a hand rehabilitation exoskeleton that achieves at least 98% motion intention recognition accuracy using sEMG signals while maintaining backdrivability and enabling task-oriented assistance across several gesture motions.
In this study, we propose **HandMaid** , an innovative design that incorporates passive wrist exercises and flexion–extension movements to make the rehabilitation process more effective, efficient, and safe. Additionally, we introduce a special resting mode, considering that fingers in a relaxed state are typically positioned at an angle of approximately 30 degrees rather than being fully extended. While enabling these features, we have equipped the system with active backdrivability, allowing individuals who experience joint pain to manually return the robot to its original position. The unique contributions of this study are as follows:
  * It includes all finger joints in rehabilitation.
  * It applies a perpendicular force to the finger bones.
  * The design follows a circular path around anatomical joints, avoiding uncomfortable pushing or pulling situations.
  * The movement algorithm mimics the natural motion of the finger.
  * Continuous measurement of motor current allows for active backdriving or stopping in undesirable situations, enabling a return to the previous position.
  * Despite operating in a limited space, it achieves a wide range of motion (ROM), and kinematic analysis has demonstrated its effectiveness.
  * No sensors are placed on the fingertips, sides, tops, or joints of the fingers, which prevents restriction and ensures a lightweight design.
  * The wearable module has a reasonable weight of approximately 400 grams.
  * Powered by a battery and requiring no external pressure source, it is portable and self-contained.
  * It is cost-effective and can be produced using 3D-printable materials.
  * The system employs a group of sensors that generate raw EMG data, which can be purchased for around $200, along with data acquisition, wireless communication, and motor driver modules produced at a cost of approximately $150, instead of using high-cost EMG data acquisition and signal processing systems.
  * It collects EMG signals to detect user muscle intention, evaluates them with software utilizing neural networks, and transfers them to the mechanism with high accuracy.


Overall, the proposed system distinguishes itself by integrating mechanical compliance, EMG-based control, and **active backdrivability** —a key feature that ensures user safety, enhances comfort, and allows natural interaction between the patient and the device during rehabilitation.
##  2 Materials and methods
The HandMaid hand rehabilitation robot was developed to assist patients who have experienced a loss of hand functionality due to various causes. Its development involved the design of the mechanical model, the development of EMG-integrated software, and the implementation of electronic control hardware. The system comprises a rigid exoskeleton, a MYO armband as a cost-effective sEMG device, a Windows-based PC, motor drivers, wireless communication modules, a circuit board integrating a current and force sensor and microcontroller, and a battery.
On the software and control side, sEMG technology is employed to detect the user’s movement intentions. The Spyder IDE, part of the Anaconda distribution running on Windows, is used for pattern recognition. Python code transmits the output data to the driver and communication board via Bluetooth. The software on the microcontroller processes this data, controls the motors, and compares the measured current values with predefined thresholds to implement the specific active backdrive mechanism developed for this study.
Backdrivability refers to how easily an external force—such as a patient’s own muscle effort—can move a robot’s joints in the opposite direction of motor actuation. In hand rehabilitation robots, this feature is particularly important because it determines how naturally the patient can interact with the device. If the system is too stiff or heavily geared, it may resist movement, leading to discomfort or even a risk of injury, especially for patients with limited motor control or those recovering from conditions like stroke. Sudden involuntary movements, such as muscle spasms, can also create strain if the robot is not designed to accommodate backdriven motion. Conversely, systems with higher backdrivability tend to be more compliant and comfortable, allowing the patient to move more freely and engage more naturally in therapy. In our experience, patients respond better to systems that do not feel overly rigid, which also helps improve motivation and adherence to the rehabilitation process [[21](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR21 "Zhu J, Jiao C, Domínguez I, Yu S, Su H \(2022\) Design and backdrivability modeling of a portable high torque robotic knee prosthesis with intrinsic compliance for agile activities. IEEE/ASME Trans Mechatron 27:1–10")].
Figure [2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#Fig2) presents a comprehensive overview of the system’s operational mechanism. When a user attempts to move their hand, neural activity originating in the brain stimulates the muscles. This stimulation generates electrical activity within the muscles, similar to nerve impulses, known as muscle action potentials. Eight EMG sensors positioned around the arm are designed to detect these signals through the skin. The data from the sensors are sampled at 200 Hz with 8-bit resolution, integrated into the wristband hardware, and transmitted to the computer via Bluetooth. Python stores the EMG data in matrices, where an artificial neural network is trained, and pattern recognition algorithms are used to classify the corresponding movements, producing data that drive the motors.
The microcontroller and current sensor receive these data via Bluetooth. The circuit board determines the user’s intent to either continue or stop movement by comparing the current sensor feedback with the information processed by the Python software. In situations where joint locking or pain occurs during movement, users can request the exoskeleton to initiate backdrive. Notably, the EMG sensors can be attached either to the rehabilitated arm or, alternatively, to the healthy arm—depending on the user’s preference.

A linear motor with a stroke length of 50 mm and a maximum force output of 32 N was selected, as it can operate within the same plane as the finger’s motion. Owing to the design, which generates a rotational effect around the MCP joint by the motor, the resulting force and speed are more than sufficient to support rehabilitation activities. We positioned the linear motors to move within the sagittal plane, where flexion–extension occurs, covering the entire dorsal side of the hand and extending up to the wrist. It reaches its maximum extension in less than two seconds, with a speed of approximately 30 mm/s. A mechanism that rotates around the finger joints was designed, with the linear motor performing its 50 mm stroke. This configuration provides the most natural way to move the fingers using an exoskeleton, allowing the mechanism to mimic anatomical motion. Employing arc-shaped links instead of binary links ensures that the rotation axis remains coincident with the anatomical joint axis, making it a more reliable and anatomically consistent choice. Integrating a rotational motor with equivalent power in the same plane as the fingers within existing rigid exoskeleton designs is not straightforward. Such an implementation would either require motion transmission via cables or involve additional mechanical linkages to alter the motion plane, both of which would increase the system’s complexity.
When the exoskeleton moves in sync with the fingers, it is crucial to apply force consistently at the correct angle to the finger bone to avoid pushing or pulling at the joints. This is essential for both patient comfort and system stability. The displacement resulting from rotation around the MCP joint ensures that the arc-shaped structures designed for the PIP and DIP joints operate in the same manner. Each finger mechanism consists of ten components assembled with stainless steel pins and starlocks, as all parts are movable. The objective is to apply a force perpendicular to the midpoint of the proximal phalanx, since motion occurs around the MCP, PIP, and DIP joints.

All circles are centered on the MCP joint, ensuring that each component of the mechanism moves along a concentric path. A neural network algorithm is employed to process complex data such as electromyography (EMG). Neural networks are known to provide more accurate and efficient results for complex datasets like EMG compared to traditional machine learning methods [[25](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR25 "Jiang S, Kang P, Song X, Lo BPL, Shull PB \(2022\) Emerging wearable interfaces and algorithms for hand gesture recognition: a survey. The proposed neural network architecture is designed to classify user intention based on EMG signals obtained from eight surface sensors. The network was implemented using Python and the Keras library. The input layer receives preprocessed EMG data \\( X_1, X_2, \ldots , X_8 \\), which is then passed through a multilayer feedforward neural network. The first hidden layer contains 32 neurons, followed by a second hidden layer with 128 neurons. Both layers employ the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function, defined as:
$$ f(x) = \max (0, x) $$
This nonlinear function enables the network to capture complex features while mitigating issues such as vanishing gradients. The hidden layers refine the learned features. The final output layer applies a softmax activation function, which transforms the raw outputs into a probability distribution across gesture classes, thus supporting multi-class classification tasks. The output layer consists of 6 neurons representing the six gesture classes. The model is compiled using the RMSprop optimizer and categorical cross-entropy loss. Training was carried out over 100 epochs with a batch size of 32 to ensure convergence and sufficient learning. During training, the predicted output is compared to the test data using a cost function, which quantifies the classification error. This error is backpropagated through the network and minimized using an optimization algorithm that updates the model weights iteratively:
$$ \text {Error} = \text {CostFunction}(\hat{y}, y) $$
where \\( \hat{y} \\) is the predicted output and \\( y \\) is the true label. This neural network architecture demonstrates robust performance in real-time gesture recognition, making it highly suitable for wearable robotic systems aimed at hand rehabilitation and assistance.

###  2.6 EMG data collection and processing
EMG signals are collected using the Myo armband, which provides raw EMG data from eight sensors placed circumferentially around the forearm. The signals from each sensor are sampled and stored. Since different gestures involve varying levels of muscle contraction and relaxation, distinct patterns emerge in sensors located closer to the most active muscle groups. The use of multiple sensors significantly enhances the system’s ability to achieve precise results. A custom listener class was implemented using the Myo SDK. This class continuously streams EMG data into a fixed-size buffer of length \\( n = 100 \\), ensuring a sliding window of the most recent muscle activity samples is always available. Thread-safe data access is maintained using Python’s Lock mechanism. Each EMG reading, along with its time stamp, is stored and later transposed for gesture-specific segmentation.
$$ X = \begin{bmatrix} \text {Flexion} \\\ \text {Extension} \\\ \text {Resting} \\\ \text {Precision Grip} \\\ \text {Wave In} \\\ \text {Wave Out} \end{bmatrix}, \quad Y = \text {one}\\_\text {hot}([0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5]) $$
The EMG signal exhibits amplitude values both above and below the axis. Due to its nonlinear and stochastic nature, obtaining consistent results is challenging, which makes preprocessing essential. Various time-domain features—such as rectification, mean value, zero crossing, skewness, Hjorth parameters, and RMS—were tested, along with frequency-domain features like the wavelet transform. The most effective features were selected for the final model.
The Myo armband produces a digitally quantized output with 8-bit resolution, where output values range from -128 to 128. Each movement causes different muscle groups to contract at varying levels. Since the sensors are positioned circumferentially around the forearm, muscle data can be captured in a more comprehensive manner. The recorded one-second data are stored in \\( 8 \times 200 \\) matrices, where each column represents a sensor and each row represents a sample. For software development, Spyder—one of Python’s integrated development environments (IDEs)—was chosen. Similar to MATLAB, Spyder includes a variable editor, making it easier to detect and debug errors during development. Even if the debugger encounters an error and halts execution, the obtained values can still be examined in the variable editor.
Once training is completed, real-time data processing is required for the robot to respond to the user’s intent. Processing the data as soon as it is collected ensures immediate analysis, allowing the results to be used without delay. This process is crucial for applications that demand rapid response. To achieve high accuracy with minimal latency, preprocessing techniques such as rectification, averaging, moving average, and filtering were applied. By evaluating the smallest and most meaningful subset of data, more reliable results could be obtained. The moving average technique, widely used in signal processing, is employed here to smooth short-term fluctuations in EMG data. Its primary purpose is to homogenize data points, thereby enabling better understanding of trends within the data series. Moving averages reduce fluctuations, providing a clearer framework for analysis.
Figure [10](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#Fig10) illustrates this process in matrix form. The first two matrices show the rectified sample values obtained from three sensors corresponding to the same data. The final matrix presents the data recorded after calculating the average over a 10-sample window. Since the data have been rectified, no negative values are present. This process not only improved classification accuracy but also reduced the required processing power, as the size of the classified data window was reduced to one-tenth.

###  2.7 Real-time classification and decision logic
The classification model is a fully connected feedforward neural network. Once the model is trained, the system enters a loop in which it continuously acquires new EMG data from the Myo armband and applies the trained model to classify the current gesture in real time. The classification output is a probability distribution across six gestures, from which the gesture with the highest confidence score is selected. Specific integer codes (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) are transmitted via Bluetooth to represent each pose. Upon gesture detection, a corresponding command is received by the control board. Software running on the MCU processes the received data and applies the appropriate signals to the motor driver inputs. Additionally, the control board continuously monitors the current drawn by the motors in real time and actively backdrives them if the current exceeds predefined limits, indicating a potential issue.
At the classifier input stage, each gesture requires 2.5-second repetitions for use as training and test data. Given that the EMG sensor samples at 200 Hz, 500 samples per gesture were collected, totaling 3000 samples overall. EMG data fluctuate rapidly due to the high sampling frequency. To accurately classify gestures, the data must be processed in blocks; otherwise, meaningful interpretation becomes difficult.
This framework enables intuitive control for rehabilitation robots. Moreover, due to its real-time performance and rapid training speed, the system provides a practical and low-latency solution for interactive EMG-based control scenarios. Once trained, the model enters an infinite prediction loop. During each iteration, the system captures a new window of EMG data, transposes the data for model compatibility, predicts class probabilities using the trained network, averages the probabilities across the time window, and determines the gesture with the highest average probability.
###  2.8 Supported hand gestures and movements
The hand rehabilitation robot developed in this study is designed to perform four different active exercises and two passive exercises. These include flexion, where the fingers are fully closed; extension, where the fingers are fully extended; the precision grip, where the thumb and middle finger touch; the resting position, where the angle between the joints is approximately \\(30^\circ \\); and finally, wrist flexion and extension.
During the classification process, various features were analyzed, and preprocessing steps were applied to extract meaningful variables. Since the signals collected by surface EMG sensors are affected by factors such as skin moisture, sensor placement, and individual physiological differences, training data are collected prior to each exercise session and used as input for the artificial neural network.
In the conducted experiments, data from each sensor were analyzed to identify the unique muscle activation patterns associated with specific gestures. This approach plays a crucial role in improving the accuracy of interpreting the user’s intended movements, ultimately contributing to the development of more responsive and intuitive control systems.
###  2.9 Hardware
The control and driver circuit boards utilize, Tensilica Xtensa LX6 processor, MOSFET H-bridges, employing the PJA3404 as the N-channel MOSFET and the UT2305G as the P-channel MOSFET. The MOSFETs are designed for high-speed switching circuits and can handle currents exceeding 4 A. Although the continuous current of the linear motors is around 200 mA, the start-up current can momentarily reach values close to 1 A. To accommodate such situations, we opted for a MOSFET H-bridge. To reduce the number of required inputs to the H-bridge and to ensure the safety of the MOSFETs, the gate terminals are connected to NPN transistors.
An INA226 current sensor is used to continuously measure and monitor the current at the control input. This sensor provides 16-bit resolution and high accuracy data. By ignoring the high inrush current of the motors through software filtering, a threshold of 250 mA is established. If the current exceeds this value, the robot halts its active movement and returns to its previous position.
Four 2700 mAh AA lithium batteries serve as the power source for the hand robot, providing sufficient energy while maintaining practicality and easy accessibility. LA-M series micro-linear DC motor is selected as the actuator due to its compatibility with the design and suitability for portable power sources. Each motor can exert a force of 32 N and features a stroke length of 50 mm. It can reach its maximum extension at a speed of 30 mm per second, doing so in under 2 s.
###  2.10 Compliance with hand rehabilitation
To achieve success in rehabilitation, activities should begin within 2–3 days following the onset of need, and continuity must be ensured. Appropriate planning should be conducted, and patient comfort must be prioritized. Compliance in rehabilitation is an unpredictable and uncontrollable variable that significantly influences patient outcomes and is often overlooked during treatment. Several factors affect patient compliance, including waiting times, treatment duration, session frequency, and accessibility. Elderly patients and those with limited literacy may experience difficulties in remembering information and understanding instructions. To improve adherence, healthcare providers should integrate treatments into daily activities (ADLs), simplify regimens, and provide periodic reeducation. Scheduling early appointments, creating a welcoming environment, and engaging in one-on-one conversations can also enhance patient involvement. Additionally, clear communication regarding treatment expectations and distinguishing between different exercises and splints can further support patient commitment [[26](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR26 "Groth GN, Wulf MB \(1995\) Compliance with hand rehabilitation: health beliefs and strategies. J Hand Ther 8\(1\):18–22")]. Increasing the involvement of robots in rehabilitation activities can not only ease the workload of physical therapy centers but also pave the way for rehabilitation in home environments.
The kinematic equations for the proposed hand rehabilitation robot have been derived, demonstrating that force is applied perpendicularly to the midpoint of the corresponding bone. For evaluation, the mechanism was tested on healthy individuals. Three participants wore the robot on their hands and were able to control it and perform movements without experiencing significant discomfort. The movements were selected based on their effectiveness in promoting finger function recovery. They have been incorporated into the robot’s algorithm to support muscle strengthening, improve the range of motion, and maintain flexibility.
The most effective movements for hand rehabilitation include opening and closing the fingers while the hand is placed flat on a surface, finger flexion, fist clenching, independent opposition of the thumb to the other fingers, and wrist stretching. The HandMaid hand rehabilitation robot is designed to perform these types of activities; it actively supports flexion, extension, precision grip, and resting positions, in addition to two wrist movements that contribute to hand rehabilitation. Although wrist movements are not actively assisted by the exoskeleton, the classifier detects them using EMG sensor data. This prevents misinterpretation of wrist activity and lays the foundation for future developments that could enable software-based evaluation.
Finger, wrist, and forearm movements are interconnected and often work in unison during many daily tasks. Engaging in such combined movements contributes to finger rehabilitation [[27](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR27 "Anderton W, Ferguson S, Hernandez J, Charles SK \(2022\) Movement preferences of the wrist and forearm during activities of daily living. J Hand Ther 36\(3\):580–592")]. The next phase of this study will include clinical evaluations involving individuals who require rehabilitation.
###  2.11 Safety and mechanical limits
The _HandMaid_ system ensures user safety through mechanical constraints and software-based protection rather than a physical emergency stop button. Each actuator (50 mm stroke, 32 N nominal force) operates within a predefined range corresponding to 81% of the natural range of motion (ROM). Mechanical coupling geometry prevents hyperflexion and ensures that the applied torque remains within anatomical limits.
Active backdrive logic serves as the system’s primary safety mechanism. Motor current is continuously monitored by a 16-bit INA226 sensor, and once it exceeds 250 mA—indicating excessive resistance or discomfort—the controller automatically halts actuation and reverses motion to a safe position. This current threshold response replaces the need for a manual E-stop while providing faster reaction and user-specific adaptation.
The mechanical range of motion of the _HandMaid_ exoskeleton was designed for the **entire hand** , including the thumb, and determined through kinematic analysis to ensure anatomical safety and ergonomic comfort. Each actuator simultaneously drives the MCP, PIP, and DIP joints through a coupled linkage, producing flexion ranges of approximately \\(50^{\circ }\\), \\(95^{\circ }\\), and \\(90^{\circ }\\), respectively. These values were **deliberately limited** to remain within physiological motion boundaries, preventing hyperflexion, overextension, and excessive torque at the distal phalanx.
The same kinematic constraints apply across all five fingers, including the thumb module, which was specifically designed to **avoid mechanical interference** between adjacent fingers—particularly between the thumb and the index finger during precision grip or opposition. The linkage geometry and the arc-shaped trajectories of each finger ensure that, even at maximum flexion, no cross-contact or overlapping occurs between finger modules.
By distributing the range of motion in this way, _HandMaid_ maintains natural grasp kinematics while protecting the joints from excessive mechanical stress. The defined limits also serve as inherent **mechanical stops** , eliminating the need for external end limit hardware and contributing to overall system reliability and safety. The safety design was guided by widely accepted engineering and risk assessment principles commonly applied in rehabilitation robotics.

##  4 Discussion
In this study, a robotic system is proposed to assist hand rehabilitation activities, with the potential to extend rehabilitation into home environments. Rehabilitation is a comprehensive and systematic process that requires careful execution. For rehabilitation to be effective, factors such as patient comfort, motivation, belief in the treatment, and initial experiences are of critical importance [[30](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR30 "Ödemis E, Baysal CV, Inci M \(2025\) Patient performance assessment methods for upper extremity rehabilitation in assist-as-needed therapy strategies: a comprehensive review. Med Biol Eng Comput. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s11517-025-03315-z
                  
                ")]. During the rehabilitation process, patients should be provided with experiences that closely resemble natural movement. The rehabilitation robot must move in harmony with the hand, ensuring that the user does not experience discomfort. Even during joint mobilization, adherence to the natural anatomical sequencing is essential; otherwise, the user may become reluctant to use the device. The HandMaid exoskeleton addresses this requirement by preserving the angular velocities of the PIP, MCP, and DIP joints into anatomical limits during flexion and extension movements.
For effective rehabilitation, it is essential that all fingers and joints of the hand are actively supported. Mechanisms that omit certain fingers or joints may fail to meet therapeutic needs [[18](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR18 "Chiri A, Giovacchini F, Vitiello N, Cattin E, Roccella S \(2009\) HANDEXOS: towards an exoskeleton device for the rehabilitation of the hand Streptomyces fradiae. Paper presented at EEE/RSJ international conference on intelligent robots and systems, St. Louis, MO, USA, 10–15 Oct 2009")]. Similarly, designs that employ a single actuator to drive all fingers simultaneously—as if they were a unified structure—do not replicate the natural kinematics of the human hand [[19](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR19 "Loconsole C, Leonardis D, Barsotti M, Solazzi M, Frisoli A \(2013\) An EMG-based robotic hand exoskeleton for bilateral training of grasp. Paper presented at world haptics conference \(WHC\) 14–17 April 2013")]. Relying solely on flexion–extension movements, where all fingers move together, may not be adequate for restoring fine motor control. Moreover, robotic systems that enforce simultaneous finger movements limit the variety of motion necessary for comprehensive rehabilitation. Each finger should be capable of moving independently; however, it is also important to account for the anatomical connections and partial interdependencies that exist between finger joints [[31](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR31 "Akgun G, Kaplanoglu E, Erdemir G \(2024\) Neuroadaptive control of a continuum robot for finger rehabilitation. Actuators 13\(12\):1–20")]. While finger joints may seem to operate independently, they are interconnected in complex ways. For instance, although one can move the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint while keeping the others fixed, such movement often feels awkward and unnatural.
Conditions such as muscle stiffness or joint locking may require the mechanism to either stop or backdrive [[32](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR32 "Güçlü H, Cora A \(2023\) A survey on wearable hand robotics design for assistive, rehabilitative, and haptic applications. Int J Intell Robot Appl 7:1–26. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s41315-023-00282-2
                  
                ")]. Because high force must be generated using small actuators in hand rehabilitation, gear systems are often integrated into motors to increase torque, which unfortunately compromises backdrivability. To address this trade-off, backdrivability is either neglected or clutch-like mechanisms are used, which in turn require braking systems to maintain position. This leads to additional mechanical components and increased energy consumption. While underactuated systems may serve as a partial alternative to achieve backdrivability, they inherently result in larger volume, increased weight, and system complexity due to additional components.
The system proposed in this study addresses the need for backdrivability by utilizing real-time monitoring of both the motor current and EMG signals used to infer user demand. Neither the EMG sensors nor the current sensors have a physical connection to the mechanical system, thus preserving ergonomic advantages. The implemented approach enables the system to actively backdrive when an undesired condition is detected. Compared to the use of force or strain sensors, this approach provides more data and avoids the complexity of cables and auxiliary equipment.
A rigid exoskeleton design was chosen for this system due to its stable characteristics, which offer reliable kinematic and dynamic responses. Rigid exoskeletons require less maintenance, are easier to don and doff, and can be readily designed and manufactured using 3D printing technologies. Unlike tendon- or cable-driven designs, rigid structures do not require constant tensioning or cable management and offer more efficient power transmission. As a result, the proposed mechanism is capable of generating approximately 30N of force per finger, totaling up to 150N. Since the mechanism is designed as an integrated unit that covers the entire hand, it operates in harmony with anatomical joints and does not cause discomfort. The system is battery-powered, offering full portability and enabling use across various settings without dependence on external power sources.
Instead of relying on switch-like mechanisms to activate the robotic system, this study adopts an intention-based control approach. Among the various available methods, biological signals—particularly electromyography (EMG)—stand out as highly promising for hand rehabilitation, offering superior accuracy in both signal acquisition and classification. Leveraging biological signals to trigger movement enables a more natural interaction with the device and supports the rapid recovery of muscular and neurological function. When a user’s movement intent can be accurately interpreted and conveyed to the exoskeleton through high-resolution EMG data, it has the potential to offer one of the most effective strategies for promoting functional rehabilitation. The overall latency from EMG signal detection to the corresponding motion output was measured to be approximately 1050 ms, which includes the cumulative delays of data acquisition, neural network inference, and mechanical actuation.
The proposed HandMaid platform is inherently scalable owing to its modular mechanical and electronic architecture. Each finger module is independently actuated using compact LA-M series linear motors and identical current-sensing circuits, allowing replication or replacement without system redesign. All structural components are 3D-printable, enabling low-cost production and rapid customization for different hand sizes or patient groups. This design flexibility also supports local manufacturing and maintenance in rehabilitation centers or home care settings without requiring specialized equipment. As such, HandMaid can be scaled both technically and economically to serve broader rehabilitation programs or multi-patient applications. The robotic system was evaluated on healthy individuals, yielding promising preliminary results. However, a key limitation of the study is the absence of trials involving patients who require rehabilitation. To address this, future work will focus on conducting clinical studies as part of the next research phase. Preparatory steps, including obtaining ethical approvals and recruiting volunteer participants, are planned for subsequent stages of the project. Clinical trials involving eight subacute stroke patients over six weeks demonstrated that the proposed method notably enhanced fine hand motion rehabilitation efficiency by about 35%, reducing the number of sessions and overall training costs [[33](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#ref-CR33 "Huang X, Naghdy F, Naghdy G, Du H \(2017\) Clinical effectiveness of combined virtual reality and robot assisted fine hand motion rehabilitation in subacute stroke patients. In: 2017 international conference on rehabilitation robotics \(ICORR\), pp 511–515. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1109/ICORR.2017.8009299
                  
                ")].
Regarding long-term usability, the system was designed to operate reliably under repeated daily use in non-clinical environments. The exoskeleton’s rigid frame and low-maintenance linear actuators minimize wear and mechanical backlash, while the active backdrive mechanism prevents overloading during involuntary contractions, extending component life span. The EMG model’s retraining capability before each session ensures consistent performance despite electrode placement drift or skin condition changes. Together, these features make HandMaid suitable for continuous, home-based rehabilitation, reducing therapist supervision requirements and promoting patient independence over extended periods.
##  5 Conclusion
The HandMaid rehabilitation robot is a lightweight, cost-effective exercise device designed using 3D-printable materials. Figure [17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11370-026-00697-3#Fig17) illustrates the prototype from various angles. The device can be easily donned and removed within seconds. Trials conducted with healthy individuals, along with kinematic analyses and simulation results, demonstrate the system’s potential for use in rehabilitation settings. The design prioritizes user comfort, confidence, and therapeutic effectiveness.
The robot offers direct control over the three joints in each finger. It utilizes electromyography (EMG) signals to accurately detect the user’s muscular intent and transmits this information to the robotic system. For six distinct hand gestures, it has surpassed previously reported classification accuracies. Once neural activity is detected, actuators can position the fingers in the desired posture in under two seconds. This system encourages repetitive motion through continuous exercise, thereby supporting improvements in both muscular and neural function.
Rehabilitation generally involves repetitive exercises, which may be performed under the supervision of a therapist using traditional methods or with the assistance of robotic systems. To support the mechanical structure, a kinematic diagram, equations, a mathematical model, and position and displacement analyses have been developed. The system is controlled via EMG signals. Biological data are wirelessly transmitted to a computer, processed, and sent to a custom-designed control board, which then drives the motors. The platform features its own integrated EMG data acquisition, evaluation, and motor control units.
The total weight of the hand module is approximately 400 grams—an acceptable value for a robotic system equipped with five actuators. At the time this study was conducted, the total system cost was estimated to be around 350 USD. This research successfully presents a low-cost robotic hand rehabilitation solution that offers several advantages over conventional methods. The system supports both therapist-supervised and home-based rehabilitation, with high precision in detecting the user’s movement intent. It is capable of generating sufficient force for rehabilitation activities and is fully portable. It is also capable of processing data from current sensors and, in emergency situations, activating an active backdriving mechanism specifically developed for this study.
The developed software and hardware environment for HandMaid demonstrates an efficient and responsive pipeline for classifying complex EMG patterns associated with hand gestures. It leverages a combination of signal processing, user-driven data collection, neural network training, and hardware interfacing. The modular structure of the implementation allows for future extensions, including additional gestures.
Initially, it was assumed that the HandMaid robot’s support for finger and wrist flexion–extension, precision grip, and rest positions would be sufficient for rehabilitation. Although experiments revealed that the current algorithm is capable of accurately recognizing additional movements, these were deemed unnecessary for the intended therapeutic scope. Exercises involving flexion and extension remain a central focus in many exoskeleton systems designed specifically for rehabilitation.

### Appendix A: analytical verification of tangential equivalent force
### Theorem 1
(For single-DOF rotation about the MCP joint, the equivalent external force at point _B_ is tangential) **Setup.** Let _M_ denote the center of the MCP joint, and let the joint variable be \\(\psi = \theta _3\\). The contact point _B_ on the proximal surface is located at a fixed distance \\(r_B = \Vert MB\Vert \\). Its planar position is
$$ \textbf{p}_B(\psi ) = \begin{bmatrix} x_B \\\ y_B \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_M \\\ y_M \end{bmatrix} + r_B \begin{bmatrix} \cos \psi \\\ \sin \psi \end{bmatrix}. $$
**(1) Jacobian of point** _B_ **.**
$$ \textbf{J}_B(\psi ) = \frac{\partial \textbf{p}_B}{\partial \psi } = r_B \begin{bmatrix} -\sin \psi \\\ \cos \psi \end{bmatrix} = r_B \, \hat{\textbf{t}}_B. $$
Here, \\(\hat{\textbf{t}}_B = [-\sin \psi ,\, \cos \psi ]^T\\) is the unit tangential vector, and \\(\hat{\textbf{n}}_B = [\cos \psi ,\, \sin \psi ]^T\\) is the unit normal vector such that \\(\hat{\textbf{t}}_B \perp \hat{\textbf{n}}_B\\).
Thus, the instantaneous displacement of _B_ is
$$ \delta \textbf{p}_B = \textbf{J}_B \, \delta \psi = r_B \, \hat{\textbf{t}}_B \, \delta \psi , $$
which is always tangential to the circular trajectory.
**(2) Virtual work equivalence.** Let \\(\textbf{F}_B\\) be the external force applied at _B_ , and \\(\tau _M\\) the torque about the MCP joint. The virtual work principle gives
$$ \delta W = \textbf{F}_B^T \, \delta \textbf{p}_B = \tau _M \, \delta \psi \quad \Rightarrow \quad \tau _M = \textbf{J}_B^T \textbf{F}_B. $$
Substituting the Jacobian:
$$ \tau _M = (r_B \hat{\textbf{t}}_B)^T \textbf{F}_B = r_B (\hat{\textbf{t}}_B \cdot \textbf{F}_B). $$
Decomposing the force:
$$ \textbf{F}_B = F_{t,B}\,\hat{\textbf{t}}_B + F_{n,B}\,\hat{\textbf{n}}_B, $$
and since \\(\hat{\textbf{t}}_B \cdot \hat{\textbf{n}}_B = 0\\), we have
$$ \tau _M = r_B F_{t,B}, \qquad \text {while } F_{n,B} \text { produces no moment (no work).} $$
**(3) Minimum-norm equivalent force.** The static equilibrium condition is
$$ \text {find } \textbf{F}_B \text { such that } \textbf{J}_B^T \textbf{F}_B = \tau _M. $$
This single constraint yields the minimum-norm solution:
$$ \textbf{F}_B^{\star } = \textbf{J}_B (\textbf{J}_B^T \textbf{J}_B)^{-1} \tau _M = \frac{\tau _M}{r_B} \, \hat{\textbf{t}}_B. $$
Hence, the equivalent external force transmitted by the actuator to the mechanism is purely tangential:
$$ \boxed { \textbf{F}_B^{\star } = \frac{\tau _M}{r_B}\,\hat{\textbf{t}}_B, \qquad \textbf{F}_B^{\star } \perp \hat{\textbf{n}}_B. } $$
This analytical result confirms that the actuator-generated equivalent force at the proximal midpoint acts perpendicularly, producing pure rotational torque about the MCP joint while avoiding axial or compressive loading along the phalanx.
