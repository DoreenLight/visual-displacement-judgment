# Voluntary Assist-as-Needed Controller for an Ankle Power-Assist Rehabilitation Robot

Renyu Yang , Zhihang Shen, Yueling Lyu, Yu Zhuang, Le Li , and Rong Song , Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract—Objective: Although existing assist-as-needed (AAN) controllers have been designed to adapt the robotic assistance to patients’ movement performance, they ignore patient’s active participation. This study proposed a voluntary AAN (VAAN) controller considering both movement performance and active participation for an ankle rehabilitation robot. Methods: According to the trajectory tracking error of the human-robot cooperation movement, the controller can switch among four working modes, including robot-resist, free, robot-assist, and robot-dominant mode. In order to reflect patients’ active participation, the voluntary torque of the ankle joint was estimated by an EMG-driven musculoskeletal model. The control torque in robot-resist, free, and robot-assist mode was determined by the voluntary torque of ankle joint multiplied by an assistance ratio to encourage subjects’ active participation, and a stiff torque was provided in robot-dominant mode. The controller was evaluated with 2 healthy subjects and 5 stroke patients on an ankle rehabilitation robot to investigate the clinical impact on the stroke patients. Results: The experiment results showed that as patients’ disability level increased, the trajectory tracking error increased and the proportion of human-dominant time and the voluntary torque of ankle joint decreased. Moreover, the results showed that the proposed VAAN controller achieved higher human contribution ratio than that of previous studies. Conclusion: The proposed VAAN controller can adapt the working mode to the movement performance and promote the subjects to participate actively. Significance: Based on its performance, the proposed VAAN controller has potential for use in robot-assisted rehabilitation.

Index Terms—Active participation, ankle rehabilitation robot, human-robot cooperation, performance adaptive, voluntary torque.

## I. INTRODUCTION

ORE than 80% of stroke patients may suffer from hemimotor function and give a great burden to their family [1], [2]. Rehabilitation training can help patients regain functional use of impaired limbs [3]. Rehabilitation robotics can provide patients with repetitive, sustainable, and customized rehabilitation training to promote motor recovery [4]. Therefore, many kinds of rehabilitation robots have been designed, such as 7-DOF CAREX for upper limb rehabilitation [5], Rutgers for hand rehabilitation [6], ReWalk for lower limbs rehabilitation [7], Robot-assisted AFO for ankle rehabilitation [8], etc.

Control strategies are important in allowing the robot to cater to patients with different levels of motor disabilities. For patients with severe impairments, trajectory tracking control strategies such as the proportion integration differentiation (PID) control strategy can be applied to assist the patient’s limb to track a predefined trajectory [9]. Such trajectory tracking control strategies generally provide a consistent guide for patients without consideration of the human-robot interaction, so impedance control strategy was proposed for achieving compliant behavior between the robot and the patient by setting suitable gains of impedance parameters [10]. For patients with less severe impairments, counterbalancing assistance has been developed to support limbs weight in gait training [11]. For patients with slight impairment, a damping force was used in an upper extremity rehabilitation exoskeleton when the movement trajectory of the patient approaches the desired trajectory [12]. The problem with the mentioned control strategies is that they do not take into account the movement performance of patients with different levels of motor disabilities. Assist-as-needed (AAN) controller is proposed to provide the minimal robotic assistance necessary for the patient to complete a motion according to the movement performance [13]. Pehlivan et al. employed an AAN controller and introduced a feedback gain modification algorithm to compute the essential assistive force according to the patient’s movement performance [14]. The force-field control strategy was defined to adjust the assistance according to the patient’s movement performance [2],

[15]. Multi-mode control strategies have been certified to realize the AAN controller for rehabilitation robots [16], [17].

Patients’ active participation should be encouraged to achieve rapid neuromuscular recovery during rehabilitation training [18], [19], [20]. Most control strategies extract the movement intention based on human-robot physical interaction [21]. Compared with human-robot interaction torque, electromyography (EMG) signals may be a better way to reflect the movement intention because they contain rich information about muscle activity and have a mature and fast non-invasive acquisition technology [22]. Krebs et al. proposed an EMG-triggered controller and the robot assistance was triggered only when the EMG value of the patients reached a certain threshold [23]. But this method cannot achieve EMG-based continuous control, and some studies solved this problem by building linear [24], [25] or non-linear [26] physical mode to correlate the robot assistance and EMG value. EMG-driven Hill-type musculoskeletal models, a non-linear physical mode, were used to reflect the active participation because they have a high accuracy in estimation of voluntary torque according to the physiological and anatomical mechanisms [27].

Although some AAN controllers can provide assistance according to movement performance for patients with different levels of motor disabilities [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], they ignore patients’ active participation. EMG signals were previously employed to estimate human voluntary torque and reflect their movement intention, but a fixed assistance ratio was adopted in [19], [28], [29], which cannot achieve the AAN controller. Although EMG signals were used to estimate human voluntary torque in [30], [31], in their studies, AAN controllers were employed to compensate for human torque output according to collaborative tracking performance without promoting subjects active participation in robot-assist mode and without verifying in patient after stroke. To our knowledge, the combination of realizing the AAN controller and promoting patients’ active participation is important but absent in previous studies. In this article, we present a novel voluntary AAN (VAAN) controller, which can both adapt to patients’ movement performance and promote their active participation. Firstly, according to the trajectory tracking error of the human-robot cooperation movement, the controller was designed to switch among four working modes (robot-resist, free, robot-assist and robot-dominant mode) to make it adapt to movement performance. Secondly, in order to reflect the subject’s active participation, the voluntary torque of ankle joint was estimated by an EMG-driven musculoskeletal model and their difference between the four working modes was determined by different value of the assistance ratio.

## II. SYSTEM DESCRIPTION

The ankle rehabilitation robot is shown in Fig. 1(a) and consists of an aluminum footplate, an AC servo motor (DM1B-045G, Yokogawa, Japan) with an optical encoder, a motor driver (UB1DG3, Yokogawa, Japan), a motor controller (GTS-400-PV(G)-PCI, Googoltech, Hong Kong), a torque sensor (AKC-205, 701st Research Institute of China Aerospace, Science and Technology Corporation, China), a data acquisition device (USB-6341 DAQ card, National Instruments, USA), and a personal computer (PC) with software LabVIEW (National Instruments, USA). The two ends of the torque sensor are connected to the footplate and the servo motor, respectively. EMG electrodes and an eight-channel EMG amplifier are used to collect EMG signals of subjects. The robot has a DOF in sagittal plane (plantarflexion/dorsiflexion). Fig. 1(b) shows the user interface, which provides visual feedback to subjects. To ensure the security of each subject during rehabilitation training, two mechanical limits are placed around the footplate to limit the range of angle and the output torque of the servo motor is limited in software program.

## III. CONTROL STRATEGY

The overall control diagram is shown in Fig. 2(a). The output torque of robot, $\tau _ { r o b }$ , consists of $\tau _ { a }$ and $\tau _ { c } . \tau _ { a }$ is control torque, and $\tau _ { c }$ is compensation torque for the gravity of the foot plate and friction which used a widespread friction torque model [32]. According to the trajectory tracking error, the controller switches among four working modes, including robot-resist, free, robotassist, and robot-dominant mode, shown in Fig. 2(b). The first three control modes are human-dominant mode, where $\tau _ { a }$ is determined by the voluntary torque of ankle joint $\tau _ { h a }$ multiplied by an assistance ratio $K .$ . The voluntary torque of ankle joint $\tau _ { h a }$ was estimated by an EMG-driven Hill-type musculoskeletal model, which can detect patient’s movement intentions [28]. Whereas in the robot-dominant mode, $\tau _ { a }$ is a stiff torque based on the trajectory tracking error.

## A. Control Modes

The trajectory tracking error is expressed as

$$
e = q _ {d} - q\tag{1}
$$

where $q _ { d }$ is desired angle and $q$ is actual angle.

In order to reflect subject’s active participation, $\tau _ { a }$ in robotresist, free and robot-assist mode is the product of the voluntary torque of ankle joint $\tau _ { h a }$ and the assistance ratio K. The K is negative, 0, and positive values in robot-resist, free and robotassist mode, respectively. The amplitude of K is determined by movement performance and will be designed later. The value of $\tau _ { a }$ in the three control modes is defined as:

$$
\tau_ {a} = K \tau_ {h a}, | e | \leq r _ {A}\tag{2}
$$

where $r _ { A }$ is a threshold value.

1) Robot-Resist Mode: When $| e | \le r _ { R }$ , the controller switches to the robot-resist mode, where r is a threshold value. The controllerjudges that the subject performs perfectly in ankle movement. A proper resistance can be given to train his/her muscle strength. And then:

$$
K = - K _ {r}, \quad | e | \leq r _ {R}\tag{3}
$$

where $K _ { r }$ is the resistance ratio, which can be adapted to the trajectory tracking error. The adaption of $K _ { r }$ is achieved by a forgetting factor and obtained by the following iterative equation:

$$
K _ {r _ {i}} = f _ {g} K _ {r _ {i - 1}} + (1 - f _ {g}) K _ {r, e}\tag{4}
$$

$$
K _ {r, e} = (r _ {R} - | e |) K _ {r, \mathrm{max}} / r _ {R}\tag{5}
$$

where $K _ { r _ { i } }$ and $K _ { r _ { i - 1 } }$ are values of the resistance ratios at iteration k and $k - 1$ , respectively, $f _ { g } \mathrm { i s }$ the forgetting factor and represents the contribution of the resistance ratio at $k - 1$ to that at k, $K _ { r , e }$ is the contribution of trajectory tracking error to $K _ { r _ { i } }$ and $K _ { r , \mathrm { m a x } }$ is the maximum of the resistance ratio. According to (4) and (5), the $K _ { r }$ increases as the trajectory tracking error decreases and vice versa.

2) Free Mode: When $r _ { R } < | e | \leq r _ { F }$ the controller switches to the free mode, where $r _ { F }$ is a threshold value. The subject is judged to perform well and can complete the movement by himself/herself. In this case, no intervention of robot will be imposed and K is set to 0.

$$
K = 0, r _ {R} <   | e | \leq r _ {F}\tag{6}
$$

3) Robot-Assist Mode: When $r _ { F } < | e | \le r _ { A }$ , the controller will switch to the robot-assist mode. In this situation, the subject is judged to have a bad movement performance and needs a proper assistance to complete the movement.

$$
K = K _ {a}, r _ {F} <   | e | \leq r _ {A}\tag{7}
$$

where $K _ { a }$ is the assistance ratio, which can be adapted to the trajectory tracking error. The adaption of $K _ { a }$ is achieved by a forgetting factor and obtained by the following iterative equation:

$$
K _ {a _ {i}} = f _ {g} K _ {a _ {i - 1}} + (1 - f _ {g}) K _ {a, e}\tag{8}
$$

$$
K _ {a, e} = (r _ {A} - | e |) K _ {a, \max} / (r _ {A} - r _ {F})\tag{9}
$$

where $K _ { a _ { i } }$ and $K _ { a _ { i - 1 } }$ are values of the assistance ratios at iteration k and $k - 1$ , respectively, $K _ { a , \epsilon }$ <sub>e</sub> is the trajectory tracking error’s contribution to $K _ { a _ { i } }$ and $K _ { a , \mathrm { m a x } }$ is the maximum of the assistance ratio. According to (8) and (9), the $K _ { a }$ will increase if the trajectory tracking error increases and vice versa.

4) Robot-Dominant Mode: When $| e | > r _ { A }$ , the trajectory tracking error becomes very large and the controller will switch to the robot-dominant mode. In this case, the movement performance of the subject is judged abnormal and a stiff torque is provided to ensure the safety of subjects:

$$
\tau_ {a} = K \left(\left| e \right| - r _ {A}\right), \quad \left| e \right| > r _ {A}\tag{10}
$$

where K is set to 0.5.

## B. Hill-Type Musculoskeletal Model

In this study, a Hill-type musculoskeletal model is applied to estimated real-time ankle joint voluntary torque of the subject during rehabilitation training. The inputs of the model are EMG signals and the ankle joint angle of the subject, and the output is the voluntary torque of the ankle joint. The model consists of four parts: muscle activation model, musculoskeletal geometry model, Hill-based muscle–tendon model, and dynamic model of the ankle joint [33].

1) In the muscle activation model, first, the EMG signals are amplified and normalized using the maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) level. And then a full-wave rectification and a 2 Hz bandpass second-order Butterworth filter are utilized to obtain the envelope of processed EMG signals $e ( t )$ Based on $e ( t )$ , the neural activation $u ( t )$ could be expressed as follows [34]:

$$
u (t) = 0. 9 4 8 6 e (t - d) + 0. 0 5 2 u (t - 1) - 0. 0 0 0 6 2 7 u (t - 2)\tag{11}
$$

where d, the electromechanical delay (EMD), is set as 80 ms [35]. The muscle activation $\alpha ( t )$ can be obtained by the following model referring to [36]:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \alpha (t) = a _ {1} \ln \left(a _ {2} u (t) + 1\right), 0 \leq u (t) <   u _ {0} \\ \alpha (t) = a _ {3} u (t) + a _ {4}, u _ {0} \leq u (t) <   1 \end{array} \right.\tag{12}
$$

where $( u _ { 0 } , \ a _ { 0 } )$ is the node point of the curve, and could be expressed as follow:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} u _ {0} = 0. 3 0 8 5 - A \cos 4 5 ^ {\circ} \\ \alpha_ {0} = 0. 3 0 8 5 + A \sin 4 5 ^ {\circ} \end{array} \right.\tag{13}
$$

The coefficients $a _ { 1 } , \ a _ { 2 } , a _ { 3 } , a _ { 4 }$ in (12) are determined by A during model calibration referring to [19].

2) In the musculoskeletal geometry model, $l _ { m t }$ , the length of muscle-tendon unit and $r _ { m t } .$ , torque arm of muscle-tendon unit is calculated using the following equations [37]:

$$
l _ {m t} = \sqrt {\left[ \left| \overrightarrow {O A} \right| ^ {2} + \left| \overrightarrow {O B} \right| ^ {2} - 2 \left| \overrightarrow {O A} \right| * \left| \overrightarrow {O B} \right| * \cos (q) \right]}\tag{14}
$$

$$
r _ {m t} = \partial l _ {m t} / \partial q\tag{15}
$$

where $| { \overrightarrow { O A } } |$ denotes the length between the muscle–tendon origin and the respective joint center, and $| \overrightarrow { O B } |$ denotes the length between the muscle–tendon insertion and the respective joint center. q represents the ankle angle.

3) The Hill-based muscle–tendon model is made up of a contractile element (CE), a passive element (PE), and the tendons. The force generated by muscle-tendon unit, $F _ { m t }$ , can be obtained

\tau_ {h a} = \sum (r _ {m t, i} \times F _ {m t, i})\tag{17}

For each subject, a calibration on musculoskeletal model is carried out to obtain calibrated model parameters before experiments [19].

## B. Evaluation Parameters

The root mean square error (RMSE) was used to evaluate the subjects’ trajectory tracking performance:

$$
R M S E = \sqrt {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} e ^ {2} (i) / N}\tag{20}
$$

where $e ( i )$ and N represented the trajectory tracking error at the i th sampling instant and the total sample number in the human-robot cooperation movement experiments, respectively.

The proportion of human-dominant time was given by:

$$
P _ {h d} = t _ {h d} / t _ {t o t a l} \times 100\tag{21}
$$

where $t _ { h d }$ was the time when the controller was in humandominant mode and $t _ { t o t a l }$ was the total experiment time.

The root mean square (RMS) of $\tau _ { h a }$ was used to quantify the human voluntary torque output in four working modes, respectively. The metric of each working mode was defined as follows:

$$
R M S _ {\tau_ {h a}} = \sqrt {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {w _ {j}} \tau_ {h a} ^ {2} (i) / w _ {j}}\tag{22}
$$

where $w _ { j } ~ ( j = 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 )$ denoted the total number of data points in robot-resist, free, robot-assist, and robot-dominant mode, respectively, and $\tau _ { h a } ( i )$ denoted the i th data point of $\tau _ { h a }$ in the corresponding working mode. In a similar way, the RMS of $\tau _ { r o b }$ was used to quantify the robot torque output in four working modes, respectively. $\tau _ { r o b }$ consisted of $\tau _ { a }$ and $\tau _ { c } ,$ in robot-resist mode $\tau _ { a }$ was always in the opposite direction as $\tau _ { h a }$ , whereas $\tau _ { c }$ in the same direction as $\tau _ { h a }$ . Considering the direction of torque, the RMS of $\tau _ { r o b }$ in robot-resist mode was defended as

$$
R M S _ {\tau_ {r o b}} = - \sqrt {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {w _ {1}} \tau_ {a} ^ {2} (i) / w _ {1}} + \sqrt {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {w _ {1}} \tau_ {c} ^ {2} (i) / w _ {1}}\tag{23}
$$

Whereas $\tau _ { a }$ was always in the same direction as $\tau _ { h a }$ in free, robot-assist, and robot-dominant mode, thus the RMS of $\tau _ { r o b }$ in these working modes was defended as

$$
R M S _ {\tau_ {r o b}} = \sqrt {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {w _ {j}} \tau_ {a} ^ {2} (i) / w _ {j}} + \sqrt {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {w _ {j}} \tau_ {c} ^ {2} (i) / w _ {j}}\tag{24}
$$

where $\tau _ { a } ( i )$ and $\tau _ { c } ( i )$ denoted the i th data point of $\tau _ { a }$ and $\tau _ { c }$ in the corresponding working mode, respectively; and $j = 2 , 3 , 4$

The human contribution ratio in four working modes was used to evaluate patient’s active participation [30], [31], [39]:

$$
R _ {h} = R M S _ {\tau_ {h a}} / \left(R M S _ {\tau_ {h a}} + R M S _ {\tau_ {r o b}}\right)\tag{25}
$$

$R _ { h }$ denoted the human contribution in percentage of a full contribution to the movement: a higher $R _ { h }$ indicated that subjects maintained higher level of active participation during the movement. and vice versa [39].

## VI. DISCUSSION

## A. Active Participation

Evidence suggested that active participation of patents during rehabilitation training can facilitate neural plasticity and motor recovery [42]. In this paper, we developed a VAAN controller that achieved the purpose of maintaining high-level of patients’ active participation during the movement through the following design. Firstly, the voluntary torque of the ankle joint was estimated by an EMG-driven musculoskeletal model to explicitly reflect the movement intention. Secondly, the robot’s output torque in human-dominant mode was designed to be proportional to the subject’s voluntary torque and the robot is like additional muscles under the voluntary control of the subjects. Although the tracking errors may be affected by the subjects’ different levels of disability, this design integrated their voluntary efforts into the control loop and made them realize the significance of their own effort, and thus encouraged them to make active efforts to improve movement performance [43]. In contrast, the relationship between robot assistance of the traditional AAN controllers and patient voluntary torque appeared to be complementary [30], [31], [40], [41], [44]. These controllers will take over most of the physical effort required to complete the movement when subjects exert less effort and still try to keep the trajectory tracking error within a small range even when subjects do not apply any torque [13], [45], [46]. In their studies, improving movement performance was mainly dependent on the assistance from robots, while it was mainly dependent on humans making more efforts in our studies. The increase in $R _ { h }$ demonstrated that the proposed VAAN controller can maintain higher level of active participation than previous studies.

## B. Performance-Adaptive Control Strategy

Experiments have demonstrated that both excessive assistance for patients with mild impairment and lack of assistance for patients with severe impairment may inhibit motor recovery [13], [47]. In order to adapt to patients’ needs, the proposed VAAN controller can switch among four working modes through adjusting the assistance ratio in this study. The VAAN controller switched to the robot-resist or free mode to increase the training difficulty and avoid slackness for patients with mild impairment or healthy subjects (S1, S2, H1, and H2). The $R _ { h }$ value was near 1 in free mode, even greater than 1 in robot-resist mode, which meant that they performed the desired movement mainly on their own effort or even against the robot resistance. Many studies reported that such free or robot-resist mode training can help patients after stroke improve motor function [48], [49], [50]. For patients with severe impairment, the VAAN controller switched to the robot-assist or robot-dominant mode, because training with sufficient assistance may avoid task failure and then patients’ frustration, increasing assistance might be necessary to complete the movement task [47]. Unlike [41], [51], [52], where a multi-mode control strategies with fixed control parameters of each working mode was designed for different recovery stages, the proposed VAAN controller can be more adaptive, which can not only match the most suitable working mode according to the level of motor disabilities and the recovery stages of each patient, but also can tailor the robotic assistance to him /her through online adjusting the assistance ratio base on his /her movement performance over time. The assistance ratio was not only applied to adapt to patients’ movement performance, but also related to their active participation. Despite the use of EMG-driven musculoskeletal model to estimate patients’ voluntary torque and reflect their active participation has been previously suggested in [19], [20], [28], [29], the fixed assistance ratio was adopted in their studies, which meant that the level of active participation of patients cannot adapt to their levels of disabilities and movement performance. In contrast, the proposed VAAN controller dynamically adjusted the assistance ratio according to trajectory tracking error through a forgetting factor, which made a tradeoff between best rendering challenges and improving movement performance to maximize the chance of motor recovery [53].

## C. Limitations and Future Work

The limitations that existed in this study can be given in the three aspects. Firstly, some involuntary motor activity of stroke patients, such as spasticity, might contaminate voluntary EMG signals and interfere the effect of the proposed method. The solution to reduce the contamination will be considered in the future. Secondly, both parameters in the control strategy and in the musculoskeletal model may not be optimal in this study. In the future, the control parameters can be optimized represent patients’ individualized features and a set of physiologicallybased parameters for the EMG-driven musculoskeletal model can be obtained through both static and dynamic calibrations. Finally, although the experiment results verified the feasibility of the proposed approach with a limited number of subjects, the substantial rehabilitation benefits of this approach need to be confirmed through further research on more subjects and longterm training.

## VII. CONCLUSION

This study proposed a voluntary AAN controller for an ankle rehabilitation robot aimed at both adapting to patients’ movement performance and promoting their active participation. The voluntary torque of anklejoint was estimated by an EMG-driven musculoskeletal model to reflect patients’ active participation and the assistance ratio determined by patients’ movement performance has been utilized to switch the control strategies among four working modes. The feasibility of the controller has been validated via experiments with stroke patients and the controller can potentially be applied in clinical rehabilitation.
